Storage, drought and climate change: the reliability of the canal
supply from Klein Maricopoort Dam and the role of groundwater in
A31E, North West Province


Abstract

Irrigation in the A31E quaternary catchment, North West Province, appears to depend
on a canal from Klein Maricopoort Dam, which carried little water for ten successive
years from 1984. This desktop study assessed the reliability of the canal supply
against the registered volume of 2.34 Mm³/a, the extent to which groundwater could
offset its shortfalls and the response of both to climate change, and it screened flood
risk and contaminant pathways. A monthly water balance of the dam (1936–2009) was
run with and without a restriction rule and tested against the records of 1987–2009,
and a steady-state groundwater model was run in two structures that differ in the depth
of outflow. The registered volume is delivered in full in only 45% to 77% of the years,
and without restriction only about 0.36 Mm³/a could have been supplied in every month
of the record. Abstracting the mean shortfall beneath the irrigated land lowers the
water level by about 1 to 2 m in one structure and by 5 to 21 m or more in the other. In
the first structure, about 89% of this water would come from the rivers feeding the
downstream dam. By about 2050, the reliability by time decreases by a further 4 to 11
percentage points. The 1:100-year flood passes the modelled reach almost
unattenuated, and the water released from the dam exceeds the salinity target for
irrigation in most samples. The canal supply is not reliable against the registered
volume, and groundwater could offset the average shortfall in volume, but the available
data cannot show whether this water would be new or whether it could sustain the
scheme through a long drought.

Keywords: canal supply reliability; reservoir water balance; multi-year drought;
groundwater flow model; capture; climate change; design flood; particle tracking


1. Introduction

In semi-arid regions, irrigated agriculture depends largely on water that has been
stored. Irrigators in a semi-arid South African catchment have coped with past
droughts mainly through the construction of water storage and the adoption of more



                                           1
efficient irrigation (Lankford et al., 2023). The security of an irrigation supply depends
on how reliably a dam can deliver water, which is described by how often the supply
fails, how quickly it recovers and how severe a failure is (Hashimoto et al., 1982). In the
Marico area of the North West Province, irrigation was the largest water use in 2000, at
approximately 32 million cubic metres per year (Mm³/a), and most of this water was
drawn from surface water below dams such as Marico-Bosveld and Klein Maricopoort
(DWAF, 2004:ii, 52).

In South Africa, the quaternary catchment serves as the fundamental hydrological unit
for managing water resources (DWS, s.a.d). In semi-arid quaternary catchments, river
flow and groundwater recharge do not respond to rainfall in simple proportion. Where
about 500 mm of rain falls per year, a decrease of 10% in rainfall may reduce perennial
surface drainage by half (De Wit & Stankiewicz, 2006), and groundwater in drylands is
recharged episodically, mainly by heavy rainfall (Cuthbert et al., 2019). Dry years tend
to occur in runs, and six of the eight most severe two-year droughts in southern Africa
between 1901 and 1999 ended between 1983 and 1996 (Rouault & Richard, 2005).
During such dry periods, groundwater sustains river flow as baseflow (Xie et al., 2024).
Consequently, water availability in a quaternary catchment is strongly influenced by
the sequence of wet and dry years and by the interaction between surface water and
groundwater.

Groundwater is a crucial water resource in many semi-arid regions, because it buffers
the effects of rainfall variability (Calow et al., 2010). In South Africa, groundwater
contributes approximately 15% of the water consumed nationally (DWAF, cited by
Knüppe, 2011:69), but in the Marico area it supplied 6 of the 32 Mm³/a used for
irrigation (DWAF, 2004:52). Groundwater can be abstracted sustainably only at a rate
that is balanced by recharge, and over-abstraction has already lowered groundwater
levels in the dolomite aquifers of the North West Province (Cobbing, 2018). Its use for
irrigation is further limited by the yield of boreholes, since yields above 5 L/s are not
widespread in Africa (MacDonald et al., 2012).

The A31E quaternary catchment is a semi-arid catchment in which irrigation depends
on stored surface water. The largest part of its registered water use is not groundwater,
but water that appears to be supplied by a canal from Klein Maricopoort Dam, which
stands on the upstream boundary of the catchment (DWAF, 2004:50; DWS, s.a.f). A
failure of this supply directly affects the water users in the catchment, and it raises the


                                            2
question of whether groundwater within A31E could sustain them through such a
failure.

1.1 Research problem

Klein Maricopoort Dam stores water that is generated in the catchments above A31E.
Of the 3.38 Mm³/a of water use registered in A31E, 2.34 Mm³/a (69%) is registered as
scheme use on 442 ha within about 15 km of the dam wall, while 0.285 Mm³/a is
registered from boreholes (DWS, s.a.f). These scheme records are inferred to
represent the users of the canal, because the Department lists a canal scheme for the
dam and the land mapped as irrigated lies below it (DFFE, 2024; DWS, 2024).

The records show that this supply has failed for extended periods. Between October
1983 and September 1995, the dam stood below 25% of its capacity in 139 of the
144 months, and this period is referred to as the drought throughout this study. During
these years the canal carried a mean of 0.25 Mm³/a, compared with a median of
2.02 Mm³/a over 72 years of record (DWA, 2012; DWS, s.a.b). The inflow to the dam
did not recover when the rainfall did: it stayed below its long-term median from 1982 to
1994, although the rainfall of 1986–1995 averaged 99% of its long-term mean (Bailey
& Pitman, 2016). The Department reports a yield of 1.4 Mm³/a for the dam, without a
stated method and with very low confidence in the hydrological information of the
Marico (DWAF, 2004:50, 83). How often, for how long and by how much the deliveries
of the canal fall below the registered volume is not known.

It is equally uncertain whether groundwater within A31E could serve as an alternative
source. The estimated recharge of 17.336 Mm³/a is far larger than the current
groundwater use (DWS, 2021). The mapped borehole yields are low, each borehole
has a single recorded water level, and no pumping tests or water-level series exist
(DWS, s.a.c). The only gauged stream inside A31E was dry in 42 of the 50 months
from February 1984 (DWS, s.a.b), which suggests that groundwater storage declined
during the same drought that emptied the dam.

Climate change adds a further uncertainty. Warming is projected with considerably
more confidence than any change in rainfall (DEA, 2013), and droughts are projected
to become more frequent and longer (Trisos et al., 2022). For 2041–2060, the climate
models assessed by the IPCC project a warming of about 1.5 to 2.9 °C for eastern
southern Africa (ESAF), the region in which A31E lies, and a change in annual rainfall


                                           3
of about −8.5% to +3%, with a median of about −3% (10th to 90th percentiles;
calculated from Gutiérrez et al., 2021; Iturbide et al., 2020). The problem addressed in
this study is that the reliability of the canal supply, the extent to which groundwater
could offset its shortfalls and the sensitivity of both to climate change have not been
established. Water users and the responsible authorities lack a quantified basis for
drought planning.

1.2 Aim, objectives and research questions

The aim of this study is to quantify the reliability of the canal supply from Klein
Maricopoort Dam, the extent to which groundwater within A31E could offset its
shortfalls and the response of both to climate change, so that drought planning in the
catchment can rest on the records rather than on a registered volume. The study is a
desktop assessment of existing records, intended for the Department of Water and
Sanitation (DWS), the North West Premier’s Office and the consulting partner AGES.
Flood risk along one reach of the Klein Marico River and the pathways along which
contaminants could move are screened, and the transport of contaminants is not
modelled.

The objectives of the study are:

1. To compile and review the existing hydrological, geohydrological and water-use
    information for A31E and identify its gaps.

2. To characterise the hydrology and groundwater of A31E and develop a conceptual
    model of how water moves through it.

3. To simulate the water balance of Klein Maricopoort Dam and its canal and quantify
    the reliability of supply against the registered scheme volume.

4. To assess with a groundwater flow model how far abstraction within A31E could
    offset shortfalls in the canal supply, and the limits that the data place on this
    assessment.

5. To assess the effects of climate change by about 2050 on the reliability of the canal
    supply and on groundwater.

6. To provide evidence-based recommendations, including priorities for data
    collection, to the DWS, the North West Premier’s Office and AGES.




                                           4
The main research question is: How reliable is the canal supply from Klein Maricopoort
Dam to irrigators in quaternary catchment A31E under the historical sequence of wet
and dry years, to what extent could groundwater within A31E offset the shortfalls that
arise when the dam is drawn down, and how would both change under plausible
climate conditions for about 2050? Five supporting questions follow from it:

1. How have the recorded inflow, the storage and the canal deliveries of Klein
    Maricopoort Dam behaved, and how did the water generated within A31E itself
    respond to the drought?

2. How reliably can the dam supply the registered scheme volume when its water
    balance is simulated over the historical record, and how sensitive is this reliability
    to the restriction of deliveries and to the demand?

3. What can a groundwater flow model built on the available records show about
    abstraction intended to offset a shortfall of the canal, and where would the pumped
    water come from?

4. How would the warmer and probably drier conditions projected for about 2050 alter
    the reliability of the canal supply and the availability of groundwater?

5. How large are the floods along the irrigated reach, and along which paths could
    contaminants from the land reach the receptors?


2. Study Area

2.1 Location and the two dams

Quaternary catchment A31E lies in the North West Province, east of Zeerust, and
covers approximately 602 km² (Bailey & Pitman, 2016). It drains to the Marico River,
part of the upper Limpopo River basin (Jury, 2016), within the Limpopo-Olifants Water
Management Area (DWS, 2023). The Klein Marico River enters A31E at the wall of
Klein Maricopoort Dam and leaves the catchment through Kromellenboog Dam
(Map 1). The reservoir of the upper dam and its whole drainage area lie outside the
catchment, and none of the runoff generated within A31E can reach Klein Maricopoort
Dam. The water stored in the upper dam comes entirely from outside A31E, while the
runoff of A31E flows to Kromellenboog Dam, which is operated for an irrigation
scheme in the downstream catchment A31F (DWAF, 2004:50, 57).

The canal leaves the upper dam at its wall, and the Department lists an irrigation
scheme for the dam that is supplied “from the canal system” (DWS, 2024). The dam
also releases water to the river. It was raised in 1966, and its survey of 1982 gives a
capacity of 7.07 Mm³ at full supply (DWA, 2012).

2.2 Climate

The climate of the Marico area is semi-arid, with rainfall that is “strongly seasonal with
rainfall occurring as thunderstorms in summer” (DWAF, 2004:18). The mean annual
precipitation (MAP) of A31E is 597 mm in the Water Resources of South Africa, 2012
study (WR2012), which takes its rainfall from the series of rainfall zone A3B (Bailey &
Pitman, 2016:14). The mean annual evaporation of the evaporation zone of A31E is
1 901 mm (Bailey & Pitman, 2016).

2.3 Topography and drainage

A31E falls from the high ground in the south to Kromellenboog Dam, and the valley of
the Klein Marico River crosses the northern part of the catchment (Map 2). Four
streams drain A31E. The Klein Marico River runs the length of the valley, the
Doringrivier drains the western arm and joins it below the dam wall, the


                                                6
Waterkloofspruit joins it further downstream, and the Wilgeboomspruit reaches the
river at the head of Kromellenboog Dam. The modelled naturalised runoff of A31E is
7.21 Mm³/a (Bailey & Pitman, 2016).

2.4 Geology and hydrogeology

A31E is underlain by rocks of the Pretoria Group, which are cut by diabase and partly
covered by surface deposits (Map 3; Michaluk & Keyser, 1991; Walraven, 1981).
Quartzite and sandstone, mainly of the Timeball Hill Formation, form the high ground.
The andesitic lava of the Hekpoort Formation, diabase, mudrock, shale and hornfels
make up the remaining bedrock, and surface deposits lie mainly along the valleys.
Groundwater occurrence depends primarily on geology, weathering and effective
rainfall (MacDonald et al., 2012:2). On the hydrogeological map, A31E is classified as
an intergranular and fractured aquifer and a fractured aquifer, and karst aquifers do not
occur (Map 4; Barnard, 1999). The National Groundwater Archive (NGA) holds the
borehole records of the catchment (DWS, s.a.c). The Department judged that
groundwater in the Marico can meet domestic supplies “but not bulk water
requirements for purposes such as irrigated agriculture” (DWAF, 2004:55–56).

2.5 Soils and land cover

The soils of A31E are assigned to the hydrological soil groups B, B/C, C and C/D,
which rank soils according to their runoff potential when thoroughly wet, from
moderately low in group B to high in group D (Map 5; Schulze & Schütte, 2023; USDA-
NRCS, 2009:7-2). The land cover of 2022 is classified from satellite images and is not
surveyed on the ground (Map 6; DFFE, 2024). It consists of natural woodland and
grassland, cultivated land and a small area mapped as irrigated land in the strip below
the dam.

2.6 Water use and water quality

The national register of water use records entitlements and not the actual abstraction
(DWS, s.a.f). Of the 3.38 Mm³/a registered in A31E, 69.3% is registered as “scheme”
use, 22.2% as river abstraction and 8.4% as borehole use, and 98.9% of the total is for
irrigation.

The Department has described return flows from Zeerust and from irrigation that are
“being discharged into the Klein Marico River” (DWAF, 2004:86), and it noted that their
nutrient and salt content limits their re-use (DWAF, 2004:57). Since Zeerust and the
irrigation of A31D lie above the dam, these return flows are inferred to enter the river
upstream of it. Water quality was monitored at seven sites in A31E between 1966 and
2023 (DWS, s.a.e). These records are described in Section 5.1.4 and screened in
Section 6.8.


3. Literature foundation

3.1 Reliability of supply from a small dam

The performance of a water-supply system is judged by more than the average volume
that it delivers. Hashimoto et al. (1982:14–17) proposed three criteria for this purpose.
Reliability describes how often the system is in a satisfactory state, resiliency
describes how quickly it recovers once it has failed, and vulnerability describes how


                                             10
severe a failure is. All three criteria depend on a threshold that separates a satisfactory
delivery from an unsatisfactory one. In this study, a failure is a period in which the canal
delivers less than the registered scheme volume, so that the criteria answer directly
how often, for how long and by how much deliveries fall short. Reliability of the supply
is not the same as the resilience of the irrigators, who may plant a smaller area or apply
less water when the supply decreases (Lankford et al., 2023).

A small dam can carry a scheme through one dry year, but not through a run of dry
years. The relationship between rainfall and runoff can shift during a multi-year drought
(Saft et al., 2015), so that a relationship calibrated on wetter years may overestimate
the inflow in precisely the years that determine reliability. Evaporation adds to this
problem, since the loss increases with the surface area of the water and is expected to
increase further as the climate warms, and it is often overlooked in the water
accounting of small reservoirs (Aminzadeh et al., 2024). The registered volume is used
as the threshold, because it is the volume that the scheme is entitled to receive, but it
differs from both the water gauged at the head of the canal and the water used on the
fields (Section 6.1).

The only planning assessment found for the dam is the Department’s strategy report
for the Marico, which gives a yield without a stated method, does not include the dam
in its proposed systems model and does not mention the drought at the dam (DWAF,
2004:50, 83–84). Its finding that the Marico sub-area was “in balance” while the
Reserve was not implemented (DWAF, 2004:53, 93) concerns the whole sub-area,
and it says nothing about how often the supply of this particular dam fails.

3.2 Groundwater as a fallback, and modelling with few data

Groundwater security during drought depends on the water stored in an aquifer, on
access to this water through boreholes and on the demand placed on it (Calow et al.,
2010:246). A source that is reliable for domestic supply may fail under the larger
demand of irrigation. Sustainable groundwater use should be based on capture, which
is the decrease in discharge and increase in recharge that pumping brings about,
rather than on recharge alone (Seward et al., 2006:473). Where groundwater
discharges to rivers, capture is largely a loss of streamflow, and it supplied about 0.85
of long-term withdrawals in the aquifer systems reviewed by Konikow and Leake
(2014). This loss arrives later than the pumping and continues after it stops (Leake &



                                            11
Barlow, 2013), so that a balance of recharge and use cannot show which discharge an
abstraction would reduce. In A31E, access to groundwater appears to be the main
limitation, because the irrigated land lies on surface deposits of unknown thickness in
the lower mapped yield class (Section 6.1).

Where records are sparse, models are often calibrated over short periods. Ebrahim et
al. (2019) calibrated a groundwater model of the hard-rock Hout catchment over five
years and estimated the unmetered pumping from satellite data. A model calibrated
over a few years may not represent a drought that is longer than its calibration period.
For Klein Maricopoort Dam, the monthly reservoir record covers 1936 to 2010 and
includes the long drought, and the simplest defensible approach uses this record
directly. For groundwater the records are too few to identify a single conceptual model,
and several should then be carried forward (Barnett et al., 2012:26), because
calibrated models that differ little in their heads can differ greatly in their predictions
(Troldborg et al., 2007:843).

3.3 Climate change and the supply

The projections used in this study are those assessed by the IPCC in its Sixth
Assessment Report. The Atlas of this report gives regional results for the global
models of the sixth Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6) and for the
regional models of CORDEX-Africa (Gutiérrez et al., 2021). A31E lies in the reference
region of ESAF (Iturbide et al., 2020), about 1° east of its boundary with western
southern Africa. The projections for ESAF are used because the catchment lies inside
this region, but its regional mean is weighted towards the wetter east. The clearest
rainfall signal over ESAF is a later start of the summer rains, which Howard and
Washington (2020) linked to a later seasonal breakdown of the Congo Air Boundary.
For 2041–2060, the CMIP6 models under an intermediate emission scenario (SSP2-
4.5) give a median change of about −10% in the rainfall of September to November,
and 91% of the models are drier (calculated from Gutiérrez et al., 2021; Iturbide et al.,
2020, 2022). The IPCC also projects an increase in agricultural and ecological drought
in southern Africa (Ranasinghe et al., 2021:1771).

Projections that were downscaled for South Africa are less uniform. The Green Book
projects that the warming may exceed 3 °C over the northern parts of the country by
2021–2050 under high emissions, and it projects more rain over the central interior but



                                            12
drier conditions in the north-east in most of its runs (Engelbrecht, 2019:12, 37). The
ensembles agree on strong warming and differ on rainfall. Because perennial drainage
responds more than proportionally to rainfall where about 500 mm falls per year (De
Wit & Stankiewicz, 2006), even a modest drying could reduce the inflow to the dam by
a larger fraction. The wettest tenth of the CMIP6 models gives about +0.5% to +3%
more annual rain (calculated from Gutiérrez et al., 2021). The climate cases of this
study are drawn from the median and the dry end of the projections.

3.4 Design floods and the pathways of contaminants

Design floods in South Africa are estimated by three groups of methods (Smithers,
2012:634). Statistical methods analyse observed floods, deterministic methods
convert a design rainfall into a flood, and empirical methods are based on the largest
floods recorded in a region. Statistical analysis requires a record of observed floods
that is “adequate in both length and quality” (Smithers, 2012:635), which the monthly
records of A31E do not contain. The Standard Design Flood (SDF) is a deterministic
method whose runoff coefficients were calibrated on 29 catchments (Alexander, cited
by Smithers, 2012:639; SANRAL, 2013:3-43). It exceeded statistical estimates by 60%
on average and is regarded as conservative (Smithers, 2012:639). Its results are
therefore checked against the empirical formula of Midgley and Pitman (cited by
SANRAL, 2013:3-61–3-62) and against the Regional Maximum Flood (RMF), which is
an envelope of the largest recorded peaks and carries no return period (Kovács, 1988;
SANRAL, 2013:3-65).

Contaminants are approached through the source–pathway–receptor model, in which
water is at risk only where a source releases a contaminant, a pathway carries it and a
receptor receives it (Mbae et al., 2024). The pathway through the ground can be traced
by particle tracking. The program MODPATH follows particles through the flows
calculated by MODFLOW and derives their times of travel from the porosity of the
material (Pollock, 2016:2–3). Particle tracking shows where water goes and roughly
how long it takes, but not how a dissolved substance spreads or reacts.

The literature provides the criteria, the reasons why multi-year droughts and
evaporation govern a small dam, a projected range of climate change and methods for
screening floods and pathways. It does not provide a measure of how often, for how
long and by how much the deliveries from Klein Maricopoort Dam fall short, how this



                                          13
would change by about 2050, or whether groundwater in A31E could make up part of a
shortfall. This study addresses that gap.


4. Conceptual model

4.1 The system and its two waters

The system studied is the land between two dams, together with the upper dam that
supplies it (Figure 1). Water enters this system in two ways, which are referred to as
the two waters. River flow from the catchments upstream is caught in Klein
Maricopoort Dam, and rain falls on A31E itself. The first water feeds the canal and the
irrigated land, while the second can reach neither the upper dam nor the canal. Path 1
carries the water from outside A31E through the upper dam, where evaporation
removes water but not the salts dissolved in it, to the canal and the river. Path 2 begins
with the rain on A31E and runs over the ground to the streams or down to the
groundwater. The two paths meet below the canal, on the irrigated land, where canal
water drains to the river and the groundwater, and in Kromellenboog Dam, which
receives both. The inflow to the upper dam is taken as recorded and serves as the
condition at the upstream boundary.

                                                 14
4.2 The upper dam and the canal

Water leaves the upper dam by four routes, namely evaporation, the canal, the spill
and the release to the river, and no supply to Zeerust appears in the record. Beyond
the gauge at its head, the canal is not measured. Its losses, the fields that it serves and
the water that returns to the river or sinks to the groundwater are unknown, but none of
this water can return to the upper dam. In a year of median inflow, a delivery close to
the registered volume lowers the storage, while in a wet year most of the water spills,
because the dam is already full. The storage of the dam and the inflow from upstream
respond over several years, and these slow parts of the system decide whether the
canal can deliver.

4.3 Rain on A31E, groundwater and the lower dam

The runoff of A31E is gathered by the tributaries and ends in Kromellenboog Dam.
Rain that does not run off recharges the groundwater, which is stored mainly in the
weathered zone. That this groundwater moves from the high ground towards the
valleys is an inference from four single water levels (DWS, s.a.c). Groundwater leaves
the catchment through boreholes, as discharge to the streams, by uptake of vegetation
and possibly as underground outflow (DWS, 2021).

Kromellenboog Dam receives the water of both paths. The net contribution of the
reach between the dams is mostly runoff and groundwater discharge of A31E, but it
also contains any canal water that returns to the river from the fields, less the water
abstracted from the river.

4.4 Sources, pathways and receptors of contaminants, and floods

The same conceptual model shows the routes along which dissolved substances
would travel (Section 3.4). Two main sources can be identified. The first is the return
flow from Zeerust and from irrigation and mines above the dam, which is inferred to
enter the river upstream of it (Section 2.6) and whose nutrient and salt content limits its
re-use (DWAF, 2004:57). The second is the irrigated land below the dam, where water
not used by crops carries salts, and anything applied to the fields, back to the river or
down to the groundwater. The rest of the catchment had a population of only 936 in
2011 and contains no large urban source (DWS, 2021:156).




                                            15
These sources reach the receptors along path 1 and path 2. Below the fields,
groundwater can discharge to the river only where the water table stands above the
river bed, and where it stands deeper, the water leaves by evaporation and uptake by
plants instead. Whether the water table stands above the river bed separates the two
model structures of Section 5.2.2.

Four receptors follow from these paths: the users of the canal, who receive path 1
water; the groundwater under the irrigated land, which this study assesses as a
backup to the canal and for which no quality record exists; the river between the dams;
and Kromellenboog Dam, which receives both paths. The river between the dams is an
ecological flow site whose category, C (moderately modified), is to be maintained
(DWS, 2021:130; Kleynhans & Louw, 2007:A2-11). The DRASTIC index indicates how
easily water from the land surface reaches the groundwater (Aller et al., 1987:13). The
index is a relative evaluation tool that does not replace on-site investigation (Aller et al.,
1987:11, 20), and it says nothing about the surface deposits in particular.

Floods follow the same two paths. A flood from upstream enters as spill over the wall of
the upper dam, the tributaries add floods from A31E itself, and Kromellenboog Dam
receives both. The Department identified the spillway of Klein Maricopoort Dam as “a
major risk” and the spillway capacity of Kromellenboog Dam as “a major risk factor”
(DWAF, 2004:170–171).


5. Methodology

The methodology is described in four steps, namely the data, their preparation, their
processing within software and their analysis (Sections 5.1 to 5.4). Each step deals in
turn with the water balance of the dam, groundwater, floods, and water quality and
climate. All the methods rest on the conceptual model of Section 4, and the water
balance uses monthly records.

5.2 Data preparation

5.2.1 Dam water balance

Rainfall and flow are counted by hydrological year, which runs from October to
September and is named after the calendar year in which it begins. Months that were
partly recorded are treated as gaps, and a yearly total is counted only for a year in
which every month has a value. Three periods are doubtful although their codes are
good. These are the calculated inflow of 1975 to 1980, which totals 296 Mm³ against
186 Mm³ at Kromellenboog Dam downstream, the spill record of 1977 to 1979, and the
canal record of 2018 to 2021, which repeats one flow rate (DWA, 2012; DWS, s.a.b).

The water-surface area follows from the relation between the gauge reading and the
contents of the reservoir since the survey of 1982, which gives the capacity of
7.07 Mm³ at full supply on which the reservoir record is based. Evaporation is
calculated from the twelve mean monthly S-pan depths, because 43.5% of the pan
months are estimates, with pan factors of 0.82 to 0.89 that were back-calculated from
the Department’s evaporation volumes. The release to the river is taken as the mean
gauged release in each band of storage (Figure 2, upper panel).

5.2.2 Groundwater

A 20 m digital elevation model (DEM) was made in ArcGIS Pro from spot heights and
contours. It gives the top of the groundwater model, the ground levels of the boreholes
and the levels of the drains.

The model removes water from every cell through a ground drain, which represents
seepage, springs, evaporation and uptake by plants. Two depths of the ground drain
are used, and they define the two structures of the model. In structure S the ground
drain lies 1 m below the land surface. In structure D it lies 15.27 m below the surface,
which is the median depth to water of 112 archive levels in A31E and its neighbouring
catchments (DWS, s.a.c). These 112 levels include the four calibration levels but none
of the 262 levels that are later compared with the model. The depth of 15.27 m is also
close to the mean maximum rooting depth of tropical grassland and savanna, 15.0 m
(Canadell et al., 1996), although such uptake has not been shown in A31E. The base
of the weathered zone is taken at 47.5 m below the surface.

The calibration targets are the four static water levels, and the 262 other archive levels
are kept for comparison afterwards. The recharge is the regional rate of 29.2 mm/a.
The benchmark for the fit is a water table 15.27 m below the ground everywhere.

The abstraction is placed in GIS in three ways. Placement P1 spreads it over the 45
model cells under the land mapped as irrigated, P2 over the 474 cells of surface


                                           22
deposits within 1 km of that land, and P3 over the 410 bedrock cells within the same
distance. Because the effective porosity of A31E is not known, a porosity of 0.10 is
used for the particle times, which lies within the range published for fine sand, silt and
clay, the surface deposits in which 41 of the 45 cells under the irrigated land lie
(Woessner & Poeter, 2020).

5.2.3 Flood

Flood risk was assessed for the reach of the Klein Marico River below the Doringrivier,
because the irrigated land lies along it. The reach begins at point F2 and runs 15.7 km
downstream (Map 2). The catchment at F2 covers 1 357 km², including the catchment
of Klein Maricopoort Dam (F1) and that of the Doringrivier (F3), and its watercourse
length and slope were taken from GIS.

The catchment lies in SDF basin 1, with runoff coefficients of 10% for the 1:2-year flood
and 40% for the 1:100-year flood, and its time of concentration is 16.4 h (SANRAL,
2013:3-44, 3-46, 3B-2). The design rainfall is the SDF design rainfall over the time of
concentration (SANRAL, 2013). The Midgley and Pitman formula was applied as a
check with the MAP of 597 mm (SANRAL, 2013:3-61–3-62), and the RMF was applied
as an upper limit for Kovács regions K3 and K4, on whose boundary the catchment lies
(Kovács, 1988; SANRAL, 2013:3-65–3-67). Each peak was given the triangular
hydrograph that the manual prescribes for the SDF, which rises over one time of
concentration and falls over two (SANRAL, 2013:3-45, 3-47).

The SRTM model is used for the flood model alone, because the 20 m DEM smoothed
the valleys. Manning’s n was set for each land-cover group within published ranges
(USACE HEC, s.a.b).

5.3 Data processing within software

5.3.1 Dam water balance

The monthly water balance of the dam and its canal was built in Microsoft Excel from
the station and reservoir records. It is a mass balance of one reservoir with a restriction
rule, which is the form that Adeloye et al. (2016) use to calculate reliability, resilience
and vulnerability for a single dam. A simpler storage–yield calculation would leave out
evaporation, while a river-system model such as WEAP (Dlamini et al., 2023) would
add components for which no records exist.

In each month, the storage at the end equals the storage at the start, plus the inflow
and the rain on the water surface, minus evaporation, the release to the river, the canal
delivery and the spill. The balance runs over the 888 months from October 1936 to
September 2010 and applies the present capacity of 7.07 Mm³ to every year. It
describes the present dam and registered volume under the inflows of the past and is




                                                24
not a reconstruction of history. The canal receives no water once the storage reaches
0.166 Mm³, which is the lowest storage on record.

5.3.2 Groundwater

The shares of rock group, aquifer type, yield class and soil group, and all elevations,
were measured in ArcGIS Pro by overlaying the mapped layers on the boundary of
A31E and on the land mapped as irrigated.

The groundwater model was built in GMS 10.9.6 and run with MODFLOW-NWT
(Niswonger et al., 2011). It consists of one layer, 207.5 m thick, with its top at the 20 m
DEM, and 15 142 active cells of about 200 m by 200 m (145 rows and 222 columns).
The recharge of 29.2 mm/a (17.53 Mm³/a) is applied to every cell, and the outer edge
is closed. Water leaves the model only through drains, which remove water whenever
the water level stands above them (Harbaugh, 2005). River drains follow the four
named rivers at the level of the lowest land beside the channel, and stream drains
follow all other mapped streams at the land surface, both with a conductance of
10 m²/d per metre of channel. The ground drain of every cell has a conductance of
0.05 m²/d per m².

The hydraulic conductivity has two zones, surface deposits (3 479 cells) and bedrock
(11 663 cells). It was estimated for each structure with PEST (Doherty & Hunt, 2010)
within the limits 0.03–3.0 m/d for the deposits and 0.0005–0.05 m/d for the bedrock,
using the four static water levels with an interval of 10 m each. The recharge was held
at its regional value, because recharge and conductivity cannot be separated in a
calibration on heads alone (Knowling & Werner, 2016). The calibrated model without
wells is the base case of each structure.

A31E has no measured aquifer properties for analytical solutions (Leake & Barlow,
2013) and no water-level series for a transient model. The model is therefore run at
steady state, in which the water entering the aquifer equals the water leaving it, and the
cases are compared with one another. Particle tracking used MODPATH 7 (Pollock,
2016) in the base case of each structure. Nine particles were released at the water
table in each of the 45 cells under the irrigated land, 405 in all, and tracked forward.

5.3.3 Flood

The floods were routed with HEC-RAS 7.0.1 in an unsteady two-dimensional model
(USACE HEC, s.a.a), in which the water can spread in any direction over the valley
floor as the flood passes. The model area is 24.3 km², with a mesh of 30 m cells refined

resists the flow, was applied by land-cover group. The upstream boundary at F2 is the
inflow hydrograph with an energy slope from the terrain, and the downstream
boundary is normal depth. The default diffusion-wave equations were used, and the
full shallow-water equations as a check.

5.4 Analysis

5.4.1 Dam water balance

To pass the test, the simulated monthly storage and yearly canal volume had to meet
the criteria of Moriasi et al. (2007:891) for a satisfactory simulation, which were set for
streamflow and are applied here by analogy. These criteria are a Nash–Sutcliffe
efficiency (NSE) above 0.50, a ratio of the root-mean-square error to the standard
deviation of the observations (RSR) of 0.70 or less, and a percent bias (PBIAS) within
±25%. The inflow itself cannot be tested, because it is calculated from the storage
record. For the same reason, the fit of the monthly storage mainly tests the restriction
rule and the outflows, while the yearly canal volume is the more independent test. The
test is independent only for the restriction rule and the demand, because the area
curve, the pan factors and the release table were derived from the test period itself,
and passing a split test is in any case “a necessary, rather than a sufficient, condition”
for a model to be adequate (Klemeš, 1986:13). After the test, the rule was refitted on
both periods together, so that the rule used in all further runs also contains the test
period. Its shares rise from 0.036 in the lowest zone to 1.00 above 57% of capacity.
The second, adjusted inflow series was run as a check.

The supply is judged by the criteria of Hashimoto et al. (1982), calculated with the
formulas of Adeloye et al. (2016). A month fails when the delivery is below 99% of the


                                            26
registered volume of that month. Reliability by time is the share of months without
failure, reliability by volume the share of the registered volume delivered, and yearly
reliability the share of years with the full volume. Resilience is the number of shortfall
events, which are unbroken runs of failure months, divided by the number of failure
months, and vulnerability is the mean depth of a shortfall. The shortfall of a month is
the registered volume less the simulated delivery at the head of the canal, and it is
referred to as the shortfall against the registered volume throughout. Every criterion is
a frequency counted in one record of 74 years and not a probability, since synthetic
series (Kjeldsen & Rosbjerg, 2004) would require a stochastic model of the inflow. A
curve of reliability against demand was run for demands of 0.5 to 3.0 Mm³/a. The
historic firm yield is the largest demand that the dam could have met in every month of
the record without restriction.

The records cannot distinguish a month in which the canal was restricted from a month
in which less water was wanted, and every run is made twice. With the restriction rule,
the canal receives the share given by the rule, while without restriction it receives the
full volume for as long as water remains in the dam. The unrestricted run gives the
upper bound of reliability, because every restricted month counts as a failure and much
of the water held back in the dam is lost to evaporation. The run with the rule gives the
lower bound for the scheme as it was operated on average over the record, but it is not
the lowest possible value, since a rule that restricts more would lower the reliability
further (Section 6.3). For resilience and vulnerability the order is reversed, because a
restricted canal fails more often but less deeply, and the two bounds are always
reported together. The sensitivity of the results was tested by changing one input at a
time (Pianosi et al., 2016:216), namely the inflow series, evaporation, the capacity, the
release to the river, the rule and the seasonal pattern of demand.

5.4.2 Groundwater

The shortfall was first compared with the groundwater balance of the Reserve
determination (DWS, 2021). Such a balance treats recharge as water that is free to be
taken, while pumped water is balanced mostly by capture (Section 3.2), and only a flow
model can show which discharge is reduced by pumping. With four static water levels
and a steady-state calibration, the model belongs to Class 1, the lowest class of
confidence in the Australian groundwater modelling guidelines, whose uses include



                                           27
developing coarse relationships between abstraction and impact (Barnett et al.,
2012:20–21). Both structures are needed because the records contradict each of them
(Section 6.4) and cannot identify a single conceptual model (Section 3.2). The fit was
compared with the benchmark, and the 262 other archive levels were compared with
both structures afterwards. Neither comparison is a validation (SKM, 2013:18).

Abstraction was run at rates of 0.25 to 2.34 Mm³/a at placement P1. Placements P2
and P3 test whether spreading the abstraction changes the result. The recharge was
also multiplied by the factors of cases M and D, 0.96 and 0.89, both without wells and
with 0.5 Mm³/a at P1.

Four results are read from each case and compared with the base case at the same
recharge. These are the rate actually pumped, because MODFLOW-NWT reduces the
rate of a well when its cell nearly dries (Niswonger et al., 2011), the drawdown in the
pumped cells, the number of pumped cells in which the water level falls below the base
of the weathered zone at 47.5 m, and the change in the outflow of each group of drains
as a share of the rate pumped. A reduction in the river or stream drains is water that
would otherwise have flowed to Kromellenboog Dam. The additional lowering of the
water table caused by a lower recharge is read from two runs that both include the
abstraction, one at the base recharge and one at the reduced recharge (Map 7).

A steady-state drawdown is the state reached if pumping continued indefinitely at the
mean recharge. It exceeds the drawdown of a single season of pumping at the same
recharge, but it is not an upper limit for a long drought, during which the recharge falls
below its mean. The loss of streamflow would in reality arrive later and continue after
pumping stops (Section 3.2). Two further runs show what the four water levels cannot
decide. In one, the recharge and conductivities are halved or doubled, which fits the
levels equally well, and in the other structure D is run with the conductivities of
structure S.

The particle times are those of the moving water alone, without dispersion, retention or
decay. A porosity of 0.01, within the range for fractured crystalline rock, would make
the times ten times shorter than those at 0.10.




                                                   29
5.4.3 Flood

As the drainage manual requires, the SDF was used as the main estimate and
compared with the other applicable methods (SANRAL, 2013:3-4). Because the
manual warns that the flood of a dolomitic catchment may be less than half of the SDF
(SANRAL, 2013:3-45), the SDF is treated as the conservative case. Four plans were
run, namely the 1:50-year and 1:100-year SDF floods, the 1:100-year Midgley and
Pitman flood, and the 1:100-year SDF flood with the full shallow-water equations as a
check on the default diffusion-wave equations. Two simplifications act in opposite
directions, since ignoring the attenuation in Klein Maricopoort Dam makes the flood
larger, while leaving out the inflow of the Waterkloofspruit makes the flood below its
junction smaller.

5.4.4 Water quality and climate

Each water-quality quantity was compared with its guideline value, and the share of
samples above the guideline was counted for each site. A value above a guideline
shows that the water could affect its use or its ecosystem, but it does not show that the
water is contaminated, and the shares of single samples are a screening indication
and not a trophic classification (DWAF, 1996b:84). The river below the upper dam and
Kromellenboog Dam were also compared over their common period, 2012–2023, with
a simple mixing check based on the share of the lower dam’s inflow that comes from
the upper dam.

In the climate cases, the dam was run with the inflow multiplied by 0.93 and 0.80 and
evaporation and demand by 1.064, and the reliability is measured against the
registered volume raised by this factor. The groundwater model was run with the
recharge multiplied by 0.96 and 0.89 (Section 5.4.2).


6. Results

6.1 Setting of the irrigated land and the records

A31E falls from 1 567 m in the south to 1 038 m at Kromellenboog Dam (Map 2).
Between the two dams, the river falls about 104 m over a distance of 24.7 km. The
Doringrivier joins the Klein Marico River 4.2 km below the dam wall and the
Waterkloofspruit 13.7 km below it. The water surface of the upper dam stands at about



                                           30
1 150 m, while all the land mapped as irrigated below it lies lower, between 1 093 and
1 140 m, so that a canal leaving the dam could reach this land by gravity.

Quartzite and sandstone cover 31.8% of A31E, the andesitic lava of the Hekpoort
Formation and diabase 24.4%, mudrock, shale and hornfels 20.9%, and surface
deposits 22.5%. On the hydrogeological map, 59.8% of A31E is classified as an
intergranular and fractured aquifer and 40.2% as a fractured aquifer. Of the land
mapped as irrigated, 91.6% lies on surface deposits. The thickness and composition of
these deposits are not known. The mapped median borehole yield is 0.5–2.0 L/s over
75.3% of A31E and 0.1–0.5 L/s over the remaining area, which includes three-quarters
of the irrigated land. The registered scheme volume is equal to about 74 L/s pumped
continuously, which would require about 150 to 740 boreholes at yields of 0.5 to
0.1 L/s. According to the modelled regional values of the GRAII (Section 2.4), the
weathered zone holds about 91% of the stored groundwater.

Each of the 266 boreholes with a water level has a single level. The 262 levels that are
not recorded as static have a median depth of 19.4 m, and the median strike yield of
109 boreholes is 0.3 L/s.

The soils of A31E belong to the hydrological soil groups B/C (38.7%), C (31.5%), C/D
(28.7%) and B (1.2%). The soils with the highest runoff potential lie along the river
valley, and 83.2% of the land mapped as irrigated lies on group B/C. The
Wilgeboomspruit yielded about 53 mm/a, or 7.6% of the rainfall, in 1966–1980 (DWS,
s.a.b). Natural woodland and grassland cover 87% of A31E and cultivated land 10.2%,
more than half of which is fallow. Land mapped as irrigated covers 168 ha, of which
162 ha lie in fifteen patches in the strip below the dam (Map 6). Built-up land covers
0.3%.

Half of the scheme positions of the register lie on higher ground above the level of the
dam, which a canal cannot reach by gravity. Three different quantities describe
irrigation from the dam. The register lists 442 ha and 2.34 Mm³/a, the land cover maps
168 ha of irrigated land, of which 145 ha is under centre pivots, and the canal carried
1.89 to 2.19 Mm³/a in 2022–2024 (DWS, s.a.b).

The yearly rainfall at the dam varied between 314 and 900 mm in 1967–2008
(Figure 4). Every year from 1981 to 1986 fell below the long-term mean, which is the
longest such run since 1920 (Bailey & Pitman, 2016; DWS, s.a.b). At the dam, 93% of

and about 2.0 Mm³/a when the dam is nearly full (DWA, 2012).

6.3 Reliability of the canal supply

In the test period of 1987–2009, the simulated monthly storage meets all three criteria

7. Discussion

7.1 The canal supply in drought

The two bounds represent two ways of running short of water, in which a restricted
canal fails more often but less deeply. Hashimoto et al. (1982:19) found the same
trade-off, where “high system reliability was accompanied by high system
vulnerability”. A partial delivery allows an irrigator to plant a smaller area or to apply
less water, while a dry canal does not. At this dam, restriction costs volume, because
much of the water that is held back evaporates, and the rule delivers a smaller share of
the registered volume than the unrestricted canal (Table 7). The reliability depends


                                                  48
more on this rule than on the demand, and the rule is the least certain input, since none
could be obtained and the two periods of the record give different rules. The test of the
balance supports counts of years and volumes more than durations in single months.
The record of the canal alone overstates how often the supply failed, because in five of
the shortfall years the dam never fell below 75% of its capacity, and the water balance
removes this effect by asking for the registered volume in every year.

The capacity of the dam is too small to carry the scheme through a drought that lasts
several years. Because evaporation exceeds the rainfall in every month, irrigated
crops require water throughout a dry winter of five months. The national register
records entitlements and not the actual abstraction. Half of the scheme positions lie on
ground that a canal cannot reach, so that the register most probably records a
reference point of each property rather than the position of the field. The registered
area, the mapped irrigated land and the volume in the canal are quantities of different
kinds and cannot be combined, and the results of this study describe the security of an
entitlement rather than the water used by crops.

The historic firm yield shows how little of the registered volume the dam can assure.
The yield that the Department gives for planning is about four times larger and is also
described as a firm yield (DWAF, 2004:54). It rests on hydrology that had not been
updated since 1988 and did not include the droughts that followed (DWAF, 2004:83),
while the historic firm yield of this study includes the whole drought of 1983 to 1995. A
yield for this dam is meaningful only together with the share of months or years in
which it is met.

One drought dominates the counts under both bounds, so its cause determines how
they should be interpreted. The cause of the low inflow is not known, and two
explanations outside A31E are possible. The first is a natural decline in the
groundwater stored in the dolomite aquifer above the dam, whose springs declined to
almost nil in 1986 and stayed low until 1996 (Pyburn, 2015:28, 44). The second is a
greater use of water upstream, since a well field was commissioned in the Zeerust
dolomite in 1974, and the Rietpoort Spring stopped flowing about ten years later during
a period of low rainfall (Pyburn, 2015:4). That the water of these springs reaches the
dam is inferred from the position of the dolomite. After the Millennium Drought in south-
eastern Australia, runoff as a fraction of rainfall had likewise not recovered once the
drought ended in 37% of the watersheds studied (Peterson et al., 2021:745). The


                                           49
explanations considered plausible there were declining groundwater storage and the
harvesting of runoff by small dams (Fowler et al., 2022). These match the two
explanations above Klein Maricopoort Dam. The records cannot separate the two,
which is common where gauged flows are affected by poorly defined developments
upstream (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:128). Since the rainfall of each year does not
explain the drought, its recurrence cannot be estimated, and the reliabilities remain
frequencies counted in one record. The low inflow is accepted as part of the recorded
sequence without a cause being assigned to it.

7.2 Water generated inside A31E and groundwater as a backup

In 1987–1995 the runoff of A31E sustained the scheme below the catchment during
the years in which the scheme inside it had almost no water. Since the canal carried
0.30 Mm³/a in these years, little of the net contribution of the reach can have been
returned canal water, and it behaves like runoff. The failure of the canal supply was a
failure of the water from outside A31E, while the runoff of the catchment continued but
entered the river below the canal, where it could not replace the canal supply. Any use
of water inside A31E that reduces the flow of the reach, whether it is taken from the
river or from groundwater that would otherwise have reached it, reduces the supply of
the lower dam. The modelled naturalised runoff shows no reduction during the
drought, which agrees with the finding that runoff during a long drought falls below
what the earlier rainfall–runoff relationship predicts (Saft et al., 2015). In a climate
where evaporation exceeds the rainfall in every month, the soils are seldom thoroughly
wet, which agrees with the low yield of the Wilgeboomspruit. The Wilgeboomspruit
further shows that the store feeding a small stream can empty within three years of
below-average rainfall and recover long before the dam does. It is not shown that this
store is groundwater, because low flows in South African rivers may also come from
storage above the water table (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:127).

The four water levels do not determine the conductivities of structure D, and each
value is an upper limit, since any lower value would fit the levels at least as well. The
better fit of structure D comes from the depth of its outflow and not from its
conductivities. The regional recharge and transmissivity are not consistent with each
other at these boreholes, unless most of the recharge leaves the ground close to
where it entered, as the ground drain allows. The archive levels support structure D in



                                           50
the valleys but decide nothing on the high ground. The water levels in the valleys
contradict structure S, while the streamflow contradicts structure D, and the real
catchment lies somewhere between the two. The unaccounted 14.5 Mm³/a of the
Reserve balance may be taken up by vegetation, may flow out underground or may
reflect an overestimate of the recharge. Structures S and D also show what Troldborg
et al. (2007) found, namely that calibrated models that differ little in their heads can
differ greatly in their predictions.

The fact that the shortfall is a small share of the recharge says little about the
availability of groundwater, because pumped water is balanced mostly by capture
(Section 3.2). Structure S behaves in this way, while structure D gives the opposite
answer, because the source of the pumped water depends on where the water table
stands before pumping. In structure S it stands above the river beds, so that the rivers
gain groundwater and pumping intercepts it. In structure D it stands below the river
beds, and there is therefore almost nothing to intercept. A drain cannot add water, so
structure D cannot show a river losing water to the aquifer, and its share of less than
1% is the least the model allows. The depth of the outflow determines the source of the
pumped water, while the conductivity determines the drawdown, which the calibration
did not fix. In structure S, an abstraction under the fields is largely a transfer of water
that would otherwise have flowed to Kromellenboog Dam. The water that boreholes
can draw on lies mainly near the surface, where it is the first to fail during a drought
(Calow et al., 2010). In the terms of Calow et al. (2010), groundwater in A31E is limited
as a backup not by its availability, but by access to it and by the question of whose
water is taken.

7.3 Climate change

The difference between the two climate cases is set by the rainfall rather than by the
warming. Both cases include the same warming of 1.9 °C, which raises the
evaporative demand by a few per cent (Scheff & Frierson, 2014). The change in rainfall
from −3.1% to −8.5% becomes a change in inflow from −7% to −20%. This
amplification agrees with the finding that a change in rainfall is magnified in river flow
where about 500 mm of rain falls per year (De Wit & Stankiewicz, 2006). It also
explains why the rainfall, which is uncertain even in sign, determines the outcome
more than the warming does. A wetter outcome offers little relief, because the wettest



                                            51
projections give little more rain than the record, and in wet years most of the additional
inflow spills from the dam.

The uniform factors also leave out what the projections emphasise. These include
longer and more frequent droughts (Trisos et al., 2022; Ranasinghe et al., 2021:1771),
a later start of the rains, which would lengthen the months in which the canal draws on
the dam before the first inflow arrives, and runoff that falls disproportionately during
long droughts (Saft et al., 2015). A uniform factor keeps the recorded sequence of
years, and the inflow factor leaves out the effect of warming on the catchments above
the dam. Cases M and D are more likely to understate the loss of reliability than to
overstate it. Groundwater recharge in drylands depends mainly on episodes of heavy
rainfall (Cuthbert et al., 2019), so that a drier climate with fewer such episodes could
reduce it by more than the factor used. Under the pumped cells of structure D, the
abstraction has drawn the water table below the ground drain, so that the drain no
longer holds it, and the drier climate adds directly to the drawdown. The climate cases
increase the shortfall while the water on which an abstraction would draw decreases,
and the groundwater backup weakens as the need for it grows.

7.4 Flood risk in this reach

The design floods of the reach are uncertain mainly because of the choice of method.
Nearly half of the catchment above the reach is underlain by dolomite, all of it above
Klein Maricopoort Dam, and the SDF is probably an overestimate here (SANRAL,
2013:3-45). The Midgley and Pitman peak is consistent in direction with the warning of
the manual that the flood of a dolomitic catchment may be less than half of the SDF. No
method can be preferred on the evidence, because a statistical analysis requires an
adequate record of flood peaks (Section 3.4), and the available series are too short or
regulated. The river gauge at the dam wall has 15 yearly peaks since 2012 (largest
25 m³/s), and the gauge below Kromellenboog Dam has 47 since 1980 (largest at least
81 m³/s) (DWS, s.a.b). Both are regulated by dams and mostly record years with
almost no flow. They cannot choose between the methods, and no flood near the
design floods of Table 11 has been recorded at the lower gauge. The flooded land is
better defined than the flood itself, because the valley confines the flow and a larger
flood is carried mainly by a greater depth. The irrigated land lies on this floodplain, so
that its users face two risks of a different kind, namely a shortage of water that lasts for



                                            52
years and the flooding of their land. Such a flood is also a pathway along which soil,
and whatever lies on it, can reach the river.

Because the reach passes a flood on almost unchanged, a flood that leaves the upper
dam continues with nearly its full peak towards Kromellenboog Dam, together with the
floods of the tributaries, which the model does not add. The Department rated the
spillway of the upper dam as “a major risk” and the spillway capacity of the lower dam
as “a major risk factor” (DWAF, 2004:170–171), and these ratings predate the
rehabilitation of the upper spillway from 2009 (News24, 2011). The flooded extent of
this study is a screening and not a flood line. The model does produce 1:50-year and
1:100-year outlines, but the National Water Act requires that the layout plan of a new
township shows “lines indicating the maximum level likely to be reached by
floodwaters on average once in every 100 years” (section 144, quoted by SANRAL,
2013:11-4). Such lines decide where development may take place, and they would
require terrain accurate to well below a metre, a surveyed channel, a design flood that
can be preferred on evidence and a model checked against a recorded flood, none of
which were available to this study.

7.5 What the two waters mean for the canal users, the aquifer and the lower
dam

The water released from Klein Maricopoort Dam lies above the target range for
irrigation in most samples, and the canal, which is fed from the same dam, probably
carries water of the same quality. This water exceeds the irrigation target in its general
salt content but not in sodium in particular. It is more saline because it comes from
outside A31E and probably carries the return flows of Zeerust and of the irrigation and
mines above the dam (DWAF, 2004:57). Evaporation also removes a third of the water
that enters the dam but leaves its salts behind. Whether this salinity limits the crops
grown depends on the crops and on how the water is applied, which the records do not
show.

Canal water with a median EC of about 62 mS/m is applied on land above groundwater
whose median EC in A31E is 17.7 mS/m in seven samples (DWS, 2021:163). The
water that drains below the fields carries salt into the aquifer. In structure S, this water
reaches the rivers decades to centuries later, and an abstraction under the fields would
capture the same water that carries whatever drains from the fields to the river. In



                                            53
structure D, the water stays close to the fields and leaves by evaporation and uptake
by plants, which would concentrate its salts further. In either structure, the aquifer that
this study assesses as a backup to the canal is the same aquifer that receives the
drainage of the fields, and its quality is not monitored. Kromellenboog Dam, whose
water is kept relatively fresh by the runoff of A31E, would also lose part of this fresh
water in structure S if groundwater were abstracted under the fields, but the model
does not represent this effect and it remains an inference.

The mixing check combines the share of 1987–2009 with salinities of 2012–2023 and
ignores the concentration of salts by evaporation in the lower dam, so that the records
are consistent with mixing but do not prove it. Episodes of nutrient-rich water occur on
both paths, including in the runoff of A31E itself.

7.6 The answer to the main research question and its limits

The main research question consists of three parts. Firstly, the canal supply is not
reliable against the registered volume, and one drought of more than a decade
dominates the shortfall. Secondly, groundwater inside A31E could replace the average
shortfall in volume, but the study cannot determine whether this water would be new
water or would be taken from the rivers that feed Kromellenboog Dam. During a
drought like the long one, the required abstraction could not be delivered from under
the irrigated land in one structure, and it would come mostly from the rivers in the other.
Thirdly, under the conditions projected for about 2050, the reliability decreases further,
while the groundwater answer keeps its form but weakens in degree.

Each bound has a known cause and could be narrowed by specific information. The
difference between the two bounds of the canal supply is caused by the unknown
operating rule, and the difference between the two model structures by the unknown
position of the water table relative to the river bed. The range of the drawdown
depends on the conductivity and thickness of the valley deposits. The interaction
between groundwater and surface water rests on an inferred indication, two model
structures and a mixing check rather than on a demonstration, because no series of
water levels or low flows exists along the river.

The data did not allow more. The inflow to the dam is not gauged and cannot be tested,
and the canal is not measured beyond its head. No water-level series exists, so that
the groundwater model has no time, and no pumping tests exist, so that it cannot show


                                            54
how many boreholes would be needed or what they would yield. The thickness of the
surface deposits is unknown, the single layer applies their conductivity over its full
thickness of 207.5 m, and the western arm, a quarter of A31E, has no static water
level. No flood peak has been recorded in the reach, and the daily record of the dam
shows no spill during the flood warning of January 2011 (DWS, s.a.b; News24, 2011),
so that neither the flood methods nor Manning’s n could be tested against a flood. The
30 m terrain of 2000 has heights that may be several metres in error (Farr et al., 2007).
The effective porosity is not known, the water-quality records cover different periods,
three sites have one to three samples, and no series of groundwater quality exists.


8. Recommendations

Each recommendation names its addressees and goes no further than the result on
which it is based.

1. Plan for shortfalls that last several years (DWS; Premier’s Office). The present
    dam delivers the registered volume in full in 45% to 77% of the years, and in 32%
    to 70% under the conditions projected for about 2050, while the wettest projections
    offer no counterweight. Drought plans should treat the registered volume as a
    volume that the dam cannot assure, and they should be tested against the dry end
    of the projections rather than the median (Sections 6.3, 6.6 and 7.3).

2. Decide on the restriction rule deliberately and make it known to the irrigators
    (DWS). The rule affects the reliability more than the demand does, and the water
    balance of this study can be used to compare candidate rules before one is
    adopted (Section 7.1).

3. Regard groundwater as a partial backup at most, and assess any abstraction in
    the valley for its effect on the river (DWS; Premier’s Office; AGES). The catchment-
    scale balance of recharge and use is no evidence that groundwater is available to
    the scheme, since in one structure of the model about nine-tenths of an abstraction
    is taken from the rivers that supplied 98% of the inflow to Kromellenboog Dam
    during the drought. If boreholes are investigated, they should be spread over the
    valley deposits rather than concentrated under the fields, which lowers the
    drawdown many times in both structures (Sections 6.5 and 7.2).




                                           55
4. Determine the 1:50-year and 1:100-year flood lines for the Klein Marico River
    between the two dams (DWS; Premier’s Office). These lines and the present state
    of both spillways should be known before further development takes place on the
    floodplain (Section 7.4).

5. Monitor the quality of the water at the head of the canal and in boreholes below the
    irrigated land (DWS; AGES). The canal has never been sampled, and no record of
    groundwater quality exists under the fields, whose drainage enters the aquifer that
    was assessed as a backup (Section 7.5).

6. Collect the data that would narrow the answer most (all addressees). The first four
    priorities, in order, are:

    (a) The operating and restriction rule of the scheme, with yearly records of the
        water ordered and delivered.
    (b) Water levels and electrical conductivity in boreholes beside the Klein Marico
        River below the dam.
    (c) The archive’s lithological logs for the valley deposits, and pumping tests in
        those deposits.
    (d) Water-level series in the valley and in the western arm.


9. Conclusions

The records show that the canal supply from Klein Maricopoort Dam has failed for long
periods. After the dam was raised, the canal carried less than the registered volume in
almost two-thirds of the countable years, and for ten successive years from 1984 it
carried little water. The inflow to the dam stayed low for thirteen years although the
rainfall had recovered, for a reason that probably lies upstream but has not been
established. The water generated within A31E decreased far less during the drought
and sustained the scheme downstream, but it enters the river below the canal and
could not replace the canal supply.

A monthly water balance of the dam, tested against the records of 1987–2009, shows
that the present dam delivers the registered volume in full in just under half to three-
quarters of the years. Without restriction, it could have supplied about 15% of that
volume in every month of the record. The result depends more on whether deliveries
are restricted as the dam falls than on the demand, and this rule is not known.


                                          56
A groundwater flow model in two structures shows that an abstraction equal to the
mean shortfall is small compared with the recharge and can be delivered in both
structures. The structures disagree on the drawdown and on whether the water comes
from the rivers or from a deep outflow, and four static water levels cannot decide
between them.

Under the warmer and probably drier conditions projected for about 2050, the reliability
decreases and the shortfalls become longer, modestly at the median of the projections
and markedly at their dry end, and these cases probably understate the loss. The
discharge of groundwater to the rivers decreases by about as much as the recharge.

The 1:100-year flood passes through the modelled reach almost unchanged and
covers about 40% of the cultivated land in it, whichever method is used. The water
released from the dam exceeds the salinity target for irrigation in most samples, unlike
the fresh runoff of A31E, and the aquifer assessed as a backup to the canal is the
same aquifer that receives the drainage of the fields.

The canal supply to A31E is not reliable against the volume registered for it, and its
main weakness is a drought of many years whose recurrence cannot be given a
probability. Groundwater could offset the average shortfall in volume, but it cannot yet
be determined whether this water would be new water or the river’s own, or whether it
could sustain the scheme through such a drought.
