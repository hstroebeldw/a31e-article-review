## 8. Recommendations

Each recommendation names its addressees and goes no further than the result on which it is based.

1. **Plan for shortfalls that last several years (DWS; Premier's Office).** Drought plans should treat the registered volume as a volume that the dam cannot assure. They should be tested against the dry end of the climate projections and not the median (Sections 6.3 and 6.6).

2. **Decide on the restriction rule deliberately and make it known to the irrigators (DWS).** The rule affects the reliability more than the demand does, and the water balance of this study can be used to compare candidate rules before one is adopted (Section 6.3).

3. **Regard groundwater as a partial backup at most, and assess any abstraction in the valley for its effect on the river (DWS; Premier's Office; AGES).** The balance of recharge and use for the catchment is no evidence that groundwater is available to the scheme, since in one structure of the model nearly all of an abstraction is taken from the rivers that supplied Kromellenboog Dam during the drought. If boreholes are investigated, they should be spread over the valley deposits and not concentrated under the fields, which lowers the drawdown in both structures (Section 6.5).

4. **Determine the 1:50-year and 1:100-year flood lines for the Klein Marico River between the two dams (DWS; Premier's Office).** These lines and the present state of both spillways should be known before further development takes place on the floodplain (Section 6.7).

5. **Monitor the quality of the water at the head of the canal and in boreholes below the irrigated land (DWS; AGES).** The canal has never been sampled, and no series of groundwater quality exists below the fields, whose drainage would enter the aquifer that was assessed as a backup (Section 6.8).

6. **Collect the data that would narrow the answer most (all addressees).** The priorities, in order, are:
    (a) The operating and restriction rule of the scheme, with yearly records of the water ordered and delivered.
    (b) A gauged record of the inflow to Klein Maricopoort Dam and of the water used above it, to settle the cause of the low inflow in the drought.
    (c) Water levels and electrical conductivity in boreholes beside the Klein Marico River below the dam.
    (d) The lithological logs of the valley deposits in the archive, and pumping tests in those deposits.
    (e) Water-level series in the valley and in the western arm.

## 9. Conclusions

The aim of this study was to quantify how reliably Klein Maricopoort Dam can supply the canal, how far groundwater inside A31E could offset its shortfalls, and how both respond to climate change.

The canal supply is not reliable against the registered volume. The canal fell short in most of the years that could be counted, and one drought of many years, in which the inflow to the dam stayed low although the rainfall had recovered, dominates the record. The water generated between the dams kept reaching Kromellenboog Dam, but it enters the river below the canal and could not replace the canal supply.

A monthly water balance of the dam is consistent with the records, though it is not independent of them. It shows that the dam delivers the registered volume in full in only part of the years. The result depends more on whether deliveries are restricted as the dam falls than on the demand, and this rule is not known.

The groundwater model, in two structures, shows that an abstraction equal to the mean shortfall is small against the recharge. In one structure most of the pumped water would come from the rivers, and in the other the pumped cells could not deliver the larger rates. Four static water levels cannot decide between the structures, so it cannot be determined whether the water would be new or the river's own, or whether it could sustain the scheme through such a drought.

Under the warmer and probably somewhat drier conditions projected for about 2050, the reliability decreases and the shortfalls become longer, modestly at the median of the projections and markedly at the dry end. These cases probably understate the loss, and the groundwater backup weakens in one structure.

The modelled flood passes through the reach with little change and covers part of the cultivated land, whichever method is used. The water released from the dam is above the salinity target for irrigation in most samples, unlike the fresh runoff of A31E, and the aquifer assessed as a backup would receive the drainage of the fields.

Taken together, the registered volume cannot be assured by the dam, and groundwater inside A31E is at best a partial backup whose effect on the river must be assessed before it is relied on. The recommendations therefore concern drought planning, the restriction rule, the siting and assessment of boreholes, the flood lines, water quality, and the data that would narrow the answer.
