## 6.2 The canal and the water of A31E

The inflow to Klein Maricopoort Dam, taken as the WR2012 patched observed flow, has a median of 3.94 Mm³/a over the hydrological years 1936–2009, or 3.23 Mm³/a without the doubtful years 1975–1980 (Figure 6). It stayed below both medians from 1982 to 1994, although the rainfall of 1987 to 1990 was above its long-term mean, and over the ten years from 1985 to 1994 it averaged 1.01 Mm³/a, which is less than half of the registered volume before any evaporation. The dam fell from 99% of its capacity in December 1982 to 22% in March 1984, and it stayed low until the rains of 1995 and 1996 (DWA, 2012).

Evaporation took a third of the water that entered the dam over 1987–2009 and about half in 1987–1995, when the canal carried only 0.30 Mm³/a (Table 6). The survey capacity of 7.07 Mm³ equals about three years of the registered scheme volume, and about 1.8 years once the mean evaporation and release to the river of 1987–2009 are added to that volume.

Of the 38 years of the raised dam, from 1966 to 2009, with a complete canal record, the canal carried less than the registered volume in 24 years (63%), with a mean shortfall in those years of 58% of the registered volume (Figure 7). The longest run of shortfall years lasted ten years, from 1984 to 1993 (DWS, s.a.b). In five of the shortfall years the dam never fell below 75% of its capacity.

The water of A31E behaved differently. Over 1987–2009, 5.01 of the 6.21 Mm³/a (81%) that reached Kromellenboog Dam did not come from the upper dam (DWA, 2012; DWS, s.a.b). In 1987–1995, Klein Maricopoort Dam passed on almost nothing (Table 6), and the reach between the dams supplied 98% of the inflow to Kromellenboog Dam. The calculated net contribution of the reach was two-thirds of its volume in 1996–2009, 3.80 against 5.80 Mm³/a, under almost the same rainfall. It is positive in every year and has a rank correlation of 0.76 with the zone rainfall over 21 years. The modelled naturalised runoff shows no such reduction and is about twice the net contribution during the drought.

The Wilgeboomspruit, the only gauged stream inside A31E, flowed in every dry-season month (May to September) on record before 1984 and averaged 2.1 Mm³/a. It was dry in 42 of the 50 months from February 1984, although the zone rainfall exceeded 100 mm in four of these months and reached 211 mm in February 1988. It flowed again from April 1988 and was dry again from March 1992. Its mean to the end of the record in August 1992 was 0.69 Mm³/a, a third of the flow before 1984 (means annualised from the months with a valid value; Figure 8).

## 6.3 Reliability of the canal supply

In the test period of 1987–2009, the simulated monthly storage and the yearly canal volume meet all three criteria, and the monthly canal volume does not (Table 7). The balance delivers 22% more water than the canal carried, and about half of the difference falls in 1995 and 1996, the two years after the drought, in which the balance gives more than three times the canal volume. The balance finds a shortfall in 192 of the 265 months with a valid canal record, against 216 in the record.

Over the 74 years, the canal receives the full registered volume in 59% to 87% of the months, and a year passes without a shortfall in 45% to 77% of the years (Table 8). With the rule, the canal fails three times as often, but a failure month still brings half of the registered volume, while without restriction the canal then receives an eighth. One drought dominates every count (Figure 10). With the rule, it forms a single event from December 1983 to January 1996, with a total shortfall of 21.9 Mm³, which is more than nine times the yearly volume and includes 40% of all failure months. Without restriction, the same drought breaks into separate events of no more than 20 months, because the inflow of each rainy season is passed to the canal at once. The rule also costs volume: the dam holds more water and loses an extra 0.15 Mm³/a to evaporation and 0.06 Mm³/a to spill, so the canal receives 1.86 Mm³/a instead of 2.07 Mm³/a. The adjusted inflow series, prepared for the years before 1987, changes the reliability by time to 0.588 with the rule and 0.865 without restriction.

The answer depends more on the restriction rule than on the demand (Figure 11). Without restriction, a lower demand helps: the largest demand that the dam could have met in every month of the record is about 0.36 Mm³/a, since no month fails at 0.36 Mm³/a and one month fails at 0.38 Mm³/a. At 1.5 Mm³/a the unrestricted canal fails in 8% of the months. With the rule, a firm yield cannot be defined, because the rule restricts the canal whenever the dam falls into its low zones. Even at a demand of 0.5 Mm³/a, the rule, as fitted to the registered volume, leaves a reliability by time of 0.82 and an event of 128 months. The difference between the two bounds at the registered volume, 0.28 in reliability by time, is about as large as the effect of raising the demand from 0.5 to 3.0 Mm³/a with the rule (0.29).

## 7.1 The canal supply in drought

The two bounds represent two ways of running short of water, in which a restricted canal fails more often but less deeply. Hashimoto et al. (1982:19) found the same trade-off, where “high system reliability was accompanied by high system vulnerability”. At this dam the trade is not free, because part of the water that the rule holds back is lost to evaporation and spill, so restriction changes when the shortfall occurs and makes it larger overall. Which bound describes the scheme depends on how it was operated, and the rule is the least certain input of the study: none could be obtained, and the two periods of the record give different rules. The result is therefore a range and not a single reliability. The same caution applies to durations. The test supports counts of years and volumes more than the length of single events, so the 146 months of the main event show that one drought dominates, but not that it lasted exactly that long.

The reliabilities describe the security of an entitlement and not the water used by crops. The register records entitlements (DWS, s.a.f), and the registered area, the mapped irrigated land and the volume in the canal are quantities of different kinds that cannot be combined. For the same reason the canal record alone overstates how often the supply failed, because in some shortfall years the dam stayed nearly full and the canal carried less than the registered volume for reasons other than a lack of water. The water balance removes this effect by asking for the registered volume in every year. It also shows the limit of the storage: once evaporation and the release to the river are taken into account, the capacity of the dam is too small to carry the scheme through a drought that lasts several years.

The historic firm yield is about a quarter of the 1.4 Mm³/a that the Department gives as the yield of the dam (DWAF, 2004:50), but the two cannot be assumed to be alike. The Department reports that the hydrological information of the Marico has not been updated since the agreement of 1988 and is of very low confidence (DWAF, 2004:83), while the historic firm yield includes the whole drought of 1983 to 1995. A yield for this dam is meaningful only together with the share of months or years in which it is met.

One drought dominates the counts under both bounds, so its cause determines how they should be interpreted. The cause of the low inflow is not known, and two explanations outside A31E are possible. The first is a natural decline in the groundwater stored in the dolomite aquifer above the dam: springs of the Zeerust dolomite declined to almost nil in 1986 and stayed low until the rains of 1996, in a period of little recharge (Pyburn, 2015:45, 113). The second is a greater use of water upstream, since the Rietpoort Well Field was commissioned in 1974 and the Rietpoort Spring ceased flowing ten years later, during a period of low rainfall (Pyburn, 2015:5). That the water of these springs reaches the dam is inferred from the position of the dolomite. After the Millennium Drought in south-eastern Australia, runoff as a fraction of rainfall had not recovered by 2017 in 37% of 161 catchments in Victoria (Peterson et al., 2021:745). Fowler et al. (2022) rated declining groundwater storage among the plausible explanations, but runoff harvesting by farm dams only as a possible contributor, since the dams are not numerous or large enough. The records cannot separate the two explanations above Klein Maricopoort Dam, which is common where gauged flows are affected by poorly defined developments upstream (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:128). Because the rainfall alone does not explain the low inflow after 1986, the recurrence of the drought cannot be estimated, and the reliabilities remain frequencies counted in one record. The low inflow is accepted as part of the recorded sequence without a cause being assigned to it.
