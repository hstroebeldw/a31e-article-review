## 7. Discussion

The canal supply cannot be relied on for the registered volume. The dam holds less than two years of that volume once evaporation and the release to the river are counted, the drought on record lasted twelve years, and the inflow in those years was about a third of the registered volume. Restricting the canal as the dam empties spreads the shortfall into more frequent but shallower failures, a trade-off that Hashimoto et al. (1982:19) found in a simulated reservoir, and it costs water through greater evaporation and spill. How much the rule matters depends on the period used to fit it, because the records cannot separate the restriction by the operator from the irrigators asking for less. The yield that the dam could have given in every month of the record is about a quarter of the 1.4 Mm³/a that the Department gives (DWAF, 2004:50, 54). The cause of the low inflow is not settled. It is consistent with recharge staying low after the dry years of 1982 to 1985 (Pyburn, 2015:45; an earlier study cited by Pyburn, 2015:113) or with greater use of water upstream (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:128).

The drought also shows that A31E holds two waters that behave differently. The water that the dam releases comes from outside A31E. It failed in the drought, and it is saltier than the irrigation target in most samples, probably because it carries return flows from upstream (DWAF, 2004:57) and loses a third of its volume to evaporation, which leaves the salts behind. The water generated inside A31E fell less in the drought and is fresh, but it enters the river below the canal and could not replace the canal supply. It sustained the lower dam instead.

Groundwater is tied to both waters, and this limits it as a backup. The shortfall is a small share of the recharge, but that says little about the water that can be taken, because pumped water often comes from less discharge elsewhere (Leake & Barlow, 2013). In one model structure, pumping under the irrigated land takes mostly water that would otherwise have flowed to the lower dam. In the other, the pumped cells cannot deliver the larger rates. The records cannot show which structure is closer to the catchment, and four water levels cannot separate the recharge from the conductivity (Knowling & Werner, 2016), so the results are a range and not a prediction. The model also cannot show river water drawn into the aquifer, so the share taken from the rivers is a minimum. The same aquifer receives the drainage of fields irrigated with the saltier canal water, and its quality is not monitored.

A warmer and probably drier climate weakens each of these. The reliability falls and the shortfalls grow longer, and the groundwater backup weakens as the need for it grows, because the recharge it would draw on decreases. The climate cases probably understate the loss, because uniform factors keep the recorded sequence of years and cannot show the longer and more frequent droughts that the projections emphasise (Ranasinghe et al., 2021:1771).

The irrigated land lies on the floodplain, so its users face two risks of a different kind: a shortage of water that lasts for years, and the flooding of their land. The reach passes a flood on almost unchanged, and the size of the design flood depends mainly on the choice of method, which the records cannot settle. The dolomite above the dam may make the SDF an overestimate (SANRAL, 2013:3-45). The flooded extent is therefore a screening and not a flood line.

For the catchment this means that the canal supply is not reliable against the registered volume, and that a drought of many years is its main weakness. Groundwater could offset the average shortfall in volume, but this study cannot say whether the water would be new or the river's own, or whether it could sustain the scheme through such a drought. Each range has a known cause that data could narrow: the unknown restriction rule for the canal, and the unknown position of the water table relative to the river bed for groundwater.
