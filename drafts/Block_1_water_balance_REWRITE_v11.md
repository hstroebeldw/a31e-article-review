## 6.2 The canal and the water of A31E

The inflow to Klein Maricopoort Dam, calculated from the dam's own record (series 1), has a median of 3.71 Mm³/a over the hydrological years 1936–2009 (Figure 6). It stayed below the median from 1982 to 1994. The rainfall was well below the long-term mean of 1920–2009 (599 mm) in 1982 to 1985 (347 to 454 mm/a) and in 1991 (314 mm), but it was above the mean in 1987 to 1990. Over the ten years from 1985 to 1994 the inflow averaged 0.84 Mm³/a, which is about a third of the registered volume before any evaporation. The dam fell from 99% of its capacity in December 1982 to 22% in March 1984, reached 2% in November 1992, and stayed low until the rains of 1995 and 1996 (DWA, 2012).

Evaporation took a third of the water that entered the dam over 1987–2009 and about half in 1987–1995, when the canal carried only 0.30 Mm³/a (Table 6). The capacity of 7.07 Mm³ equals about three years of the registered volume, and about 1.8 years once the evaporation at the mean storage and the release to the river are added to that volume.

Of the 44 years from 1966 to 2009, 38 have a complete canal record, and in 24 of them (63%) the canal carried less than the registered volume, by 58% of the registered volume on average (Figure 7). The shortfall years from 1984 to 1996 form one run of thirteen years. The record of 1994 lacks one month, but even the largest monthly volume on record would leave 1994 short (DWS, s.a.b). In five of the shortfall years, two of them in the doubtful years 1975–1980, the dam never fell below 75% of its capacity.

In 1987–1995 Klein Maricopoort Dam passed on almost nothing, and most of the water that reached Kromellenboog Dam came from the reach between the dams (Table 7). The net contribution of the reach, the inflow to Kromellenboog Dam less the spill and release of the upper dam, was lower in 1987–1995 than in 1996–2009 under almost the same rainfall, although the modelled naturalised runoff of A31E was higher in the first period. It is positive in each of the 21 years with a value, and it has a rank correlation of 0.76 with the zone rainfall (DWA, 2012; DWS, s.a.b).

The Wilgeboomspruit, the only gauged stream inside A31E, flowed in every dry-season month (May to September) on record before February 1984, and it averaged 2.1 Mm³/a from December 1965 to January 1984. It was dry in most months from February 1984 to March 1988, including February 1988, when the zone rainfall was 211 mm. From April 1988 to the end of the record in August 1992 it averaged 0.69 Mm³/a, a third of its earlier flow, while the annualised zone rainfall of the months with a valid record fell from 660 to 496 mm/a (Figure 8).

## 6.3 Reliability of the canal supply

The water balance was tested in two directions (Table 8; Figure 9). In the forward test, over 1987–2009, the simulated monthly storage and yearly canal volume meet all three criteria and the monthly canal volume does not. In the reverse test, over 1966–1974 and 1981–1986, the monthly canal volume meets the criteria and the yearly canal volume narrowly does not. The yearly canal volume is too high in the forward test and too low in the reverse test. In 9 of the 14 years of the reverse test the canal carried more than the registered volume, which the model cannot deliver. In the forward test about half of the difference falls in hydrological years 1995 and 1996, the two years after the drought, in which together the balance gives about three times the canal volume. The balance finds a shortfall in 192 of the 265 months with a valid canal record, against 216 in the record.

**Table 8 (new): Test of the water balance in both directions.** In each test the rule was fitted on the other period: 1966–1974 and 1981–1986 for the forward test, and 1987–2009 for the reverse test. The criteria are NSE above 0.5, RSR at most 0.7 and PBIAS within ±25% (Moriasi et al., 2007:891), applied here by analogy. A positive PBIAS means that the model is too low (Moriasi et al., 2007:888). The storage is not an independent check, because the inflow, the pan factors, the release table and the area curve come from the same records, and it is not compared in the reverse test, because the area curve comes from the survey of 1982. The seasonal pattern of each test comes from the canal record of the years tested.

Over the 74 years, the canal receives the registered volume in 59% of the months with the rule and in 87% without restriction, and a year passes without a shortfall in 45% and 77% of the years (Table 9). With the rule the canal fails three times as often, but a failure month still brings half of the registered volume, while without restriction the canal then receives an eighth.

With the rule the drought forms a single event from December 1983 to January 1996, with a shortfall of more than nine times the yearly volume, and it includes 40% of all failure months (Figure 10). Without restriction the same drought breaks into separate events of no more than 20 months.

The rule costs water: the canal receives 0.21 Mm³/a less on average, and the dam loses 0.21 Mm³/a more to evaporation and spill. The reliability depends on the period used to fit the rule. Fitted on the fit period alone, the rule gives a reliability by time of 0.59, and fitted on the test period alone it gives 0.39 and a longest event of 169 months.

The historic firm yield, the largest demand that the dam could have met in every month of the record without restriction, is about 0.36 Mm³/a on series 1 (Figure 11). No month fails at 0.36 Mm³/a and one month fails at 0.37 Mm³/a, in October 1992, when the dam held 0.2 Mm³. The yield is set by this one month at the lowest storage on record, and it rises to about 0.44 Mm³/a if the lowest usable storage is set at zero. At 1.5 Mm³/a the unrestricted canal fails in 8% of the months. The rule was fitted at the registered volume, so for lower demands its curve in Figure 11 shows what the rule does and not what an operator would have done.

## 7.1 The canal supply in drought

The restricted canal fails more often but less deeply, a trade-off similar to the one Hashimoto et al. (1982:19) found in a simulated reservoir, where “high system reliability was accompanied by high system vulnerability”. The restriction also costs water, because the dam then loses more of it to evaporation and spill.

A rule fitted on the later period gives a lower reliability than one fitted on the earlier period, so the result with the rule is not a strict lower bound. The operator may have restricted harder after 1987, irrigators may have asked for less water, the demand before 1987 may have been larger than the registered volume, or the records before 1987 may differ, and the records cannot separate these.

The test shows that the model is consistent with the record and not that it is validated, because the inflow, which is calculated from the recorded storage, and the other inputs come from the same records. The inflow carries the errors of every other term of the balance, but the dam stayed low from 1987 to 1995 although the canal took little water, which points to a real fall in inflow.

The reliabilities describe the security of the registered volume, and the register does not record the water actually used (DWS, s.a.f). In some shortfall years the dam stood above three-quarters full, so the canal record alone overstates how often water was short.

The historic firm yield is about a quarter of the 1.4 Mm³/a that the Department gives for the dam (DWAF, 2004:50, 54). Both are firm yields, but the Department states no assurance of supply, and its hydrological information has not been updated since the agreement of 1988 and is rated of very low confidence (DWAF, 2004:83).

The inflow stayed low from 1986 to 1990, including four years of above-average rainfall, and the dry year of 1991 fell inside the same period. Recharge may have stayed low for years after the dry years of 1982 to 1985. An earlier study cited by Pyburn (2015:113) calculated no or very little recharge in the Zeerust dolomite between 1983 and 1995, and the flow of the Paardenvallei springs declined to almost nil in 1986 and stayed low until the rains of 1996 (Pyburn, 2015:45). That these springs feed the dam is inferred from the position of the dolomite above it. Greater use of water upstream is the other possibility, and it is hard to exclude because low flows in South African records are often affected by poorly defined upstream developments (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:128). The records cannot separate the two.

The drought is a single event in 74 years, so its recurrence cannot be estimated.
