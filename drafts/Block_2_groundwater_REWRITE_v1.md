## 6.4 Groundwater model: fit and water budget

The calibration gave a conductivity of 0.430 m/d for the surface deposits and 0.0029 m/d for the bedrock in structure S. In structure D, both conductivities ended at the lower limits of their ranges, 0.03 and 0.0005 m/d (Table 10). Structure S matches three of the four static levels within 6 m, but it misses borehole 2526AD00180 in the valley by 28.9 m. With the regional recharge, the levels are matched only by a bedrock transmissivity of 0.60 m²/d in structure S and 0.10 m²/d in structure D, far below the regional 22.81 m²/d.

Of the 262 archive levels that were not used in the calibration, structure D has twice as many within 10 m as structure S, and on these levels it fits no better than the benchmark (Table 10; Figure 12). On the lowest ground the measured water table is on average 23.1 m deeper than the water table of structure S and 11.0 m deeper than that of structure D. Structure S places the water table within 5 m of the surface at 114 boreholes, where 9 of the levels are that shallow. Under the irrigated land the water table lies 4.5 m deep in structure S and 15.3 m deep in structure D (Map 8).

The two structures receive the same recharge but dispose of it in opposite ways. In structure S, the rivers and streams receive 9.86 Mm³/a, which exceeds every estimate of baseflow, from 2.00 Mm³/a in the Reserve determination (DWS, 2021) to 3.43 Mm³/a by the method of Hughes in the WR2012 study (Bailey & Pitman, 2016), and it exceeds the net contribution of the reach of 5.01 Mm³/a. In structure D they receive 0.63 Mm³/a, which is below every estimate. The Reserve determination gives a recharge of 17.336 Mm³/a, a groundwater use of 0.81 Mm³/a against the 0.285 Mm³/a registered, and a discharge to the streams of 2.00 Mm³/a (DWS, 2021). The remaining 14.5 Mm³/a is unaccounted for. The recharge is five to nine times the estimates of baseflow.

## 6.5 Abstraction to offset the shortfall

In structure S, the mean drawdown in the 45 pumped cells increases from 0.96 m at 0.25 Mm³/a and 2.30 m at 0.5 Mm³/a to 40.8 m at the registered volume of 2.34 Mm³/a, at which 19 of the cells lie below the base of the weathered zone at 47.5 m (Figure 13). In structure D the drawdown is five and nine times larger at the first two rates, 4.92 m and 20.6 m (lower limits), and from 1.0 Mm³/a onwards the cells cannot deliver the rate asked. They yield 0.93 Mm³/a at 1.0 Mm³/a and at most about 1.2 Mm³/a.

The source of the pumped water is opposite in the two structures. In structure S, 87% to 89% of the pumped water is balanced by less discharge to the rivers and streams at rates up to 1.0 Mm³/a, and more than three-quarters at the registered volume. At 0.5 Mm³/a, about 69% comes from the Klein Marico River itself and 20% from the lower Waterkloofspruit. In structure D, the rivers and streams supply 0.3% to 1.5%, and the rest comes from the ground drain.

Table 11 lists the cases that change one thing. With the conductivities of structure S, structure D lowers the water level by 2.22 m, about as much as structure S, and its water still comes entirely from the ground drain. Spreading the abstraction over the surface deposits within 1 km reduces the drawdown to a quarter in structure S and to a twenty-eighth in structure D, without changing the source. Models with half or double the recharge and conductivities fit the four water levels equally well, and they give drawdowns of 0.96 to 7.43 m in structure S. In structure D they give 4.92 m and 117.5 m, and the halved model cannot deliver the abstraction.

## 7.2 Groundwater as a backup

Groundwater in A31E could offset the shortfall only by taking water from somewhere else. The shortfall is a small share of the recharge, but this says little about the water that can be taken, because pumped water is balanced mostly by less discharge elsewhere (Leake & Barlow, 2013). In structure S the water table stands above the river beds, and pumping under the irrigated land is largely a transfer of water that would otherwise have flowed to Kromellenboog Dam. In structure D the water table stands below the river beds, so the rivers lose almost nothing, but the boreholes cannot deliver the larger rates and the water level falls by tens of metres. The model shows that the depth of the outflow decides where the pumped water comes from, and that the conductivity decides how far the water level falls.

The records cannot show which structure is closer to the catchment. The water levels in the valleys favour structure D, but D carries less water to the rivers than any estimate of baseflow, and S carries more. The catchment probably lies between them. Four water levels leave the recharge and the conductivity open, so that equally good fits give drawdowns that differ several times over. This agrees with the finding that calibrated models which differ little in their heads can differ greatly in their predictions (Troldborg et al., 2007). The model also cannot draw water from a river into the aquifer (Leake & Barlow, 2013), so the share taken from the rivers in structure D is a minimum. The 14.5 Mm³/a of the Reserve balance that is not accounted for may be used by vegetation, may leave underground, or may reflect an overestimate of the recharge. The results are therefore a range that shows how the answer depends on the model structure, and not a prediction.

In the terms of Calow et al. (2010), who linked availability, access and demand, the limit on groundwater as a backup in A31E is access to the water and the claim of other users on it, and not the volume of the recharge. Their case studies in four African countries found that complete depletion is rarely the main concern, and that shallow sources more often fail first.
