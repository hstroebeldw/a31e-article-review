## 6.2 The canal and the water of A31E

The inflow to Klein Maricopoort Dam is not gauged. It was calculated from the dam's own record (series 1), and its median over the hydrological years 1936–2009 is 3.71 Mm³/a (Figure 6). The inflow stayed below the median from 1982 to 1994. The rainfall was well below the long-term mean in 1982 to 1985 (347 to 454 mm/a) and in 1991 (314 mm), but above it in 1987 to 1990. From 1985 to 1994 the inflow averaged 0.84 Mm³/a, which is about a third of the registered volume before any evaporation. The dam fell from 99% of its capacity in December 1982 to 22% in March 1984 and reached 2% in November 1992. It stayed low until the rains of 1995 and 1996 (DWA, 2012).

The water balance of the dam shows where the water went (Table 6). Evaporation took a third of the water that entered the dam in 1987–2009 and about half in 1987–1995, when the canal carried only 0.30 Mm³/a. The capacity of 7.07 Mm³ equals about three years of the registered volume, but only about 1.8 years once the evaporation at the mean storage, net of the rain on the water surface, and the release to the river are added.

## 6.3 Reliability of the canal supply

The water balance was used to simulate the canal over the whole record. Because the inflow is calculated from the same records, the balance was first tested in two directions (Table 8; Figure 9). In the forward test, the restriction rule was fitted on 1966–1974 and 1981–1986 and tested on 1987–2009. In the reverse test, the rule was fitted on 1987–2009 and tested on the earlier years. In the forward test, the simulated storage and the yearly canal volume meet all three criteria, and the monthly canal volume does not. In the reverse test, the monthly canal volume meets the criteria and the yearly canal volume narrowly does not. The yearly canal volume is too high in the forward test and too low in the reverse test. In 9 of the 14 years of the reverse test the canal carried more than the registered volume, which the model cannot deliver. In the forward test about half of the difference falls in 1995 and 1996, the two years after the drought, when the balance gives about three times the recorded canal volume. The balance finds a shortfall in 192 of the 265 months with a valid canal record, against 216 in the record.

The criteria are NSE above 0.5, RSR at most 0.7 and PBIAS within ±25% (Moriasi et al., 2007:891), applied here by analogy. A positive PBIAS means that the model is too low (Moriasi et al., 2007:888). The storage is not an independent check, because the inflow, the pan factors, the release table and the area curve come from the same records, and it is not compared in the reverse test, because the area curve comes from the survey of 1982. The seasonal pattern is that of Figure 3 in the forward test and that of the years tested in the reverse test.

The reliability of the canal was then calculated against the registered volume over the 74 years (Table 9). With the rule, the canal receives the registered volume in 59% of the months, and without restriction in 87%. A year passes without a shortfall in 45% and 77% of the years. With the rule the canal fails three times as often, but a failure month still brings half of the registered volume, while without restriction the canal then receives an eighth.

With the rule, the drought forms a single event from December 1983 to January 1996. Its shortfall is more than nine times the yearly volume, and it includes 40% of all failure months (Figure 10). Without restriction, the same drought breaks into separate events of no more than 20 months.

The rule costs water. The canal receives 0.21 Mm³/a less on average, and the dam loses 0.21 Mm³/a more to evaporation and spill. The reliability also depends on the period used to fit the rule. Fitted on the fit period alone, the rule gives a reliability by time of 0.59, and fitted on the test period alone it gives 0.39 and a longest event of 169 months.

The adjusted inflow series (series 2) gives almost the same reliability, 0.588 with the rule and 0.865 without.

The firm yield is the largest demand that the dam could have met in every month of the record without restriction. It is about 0.36 Mm³/a (Figure 11). No month fails at 0.36 Mm³/a, and one month fails at 0.37 Mm³/a, in October 1992, when the dam held 0.2 Mm³. The yield is set by this one month at the lowest storage on record. At 1.5 Mm³/a the unrestricted canal fails in 8% of the months. The rule was fitted at the registered volume, so for lower demands its curve in Figure 11 shows what the rule does and not what an operator would have done.

## 6.4 Groundwater model: fit and water budget

The groundwater model was calibrated separately for the two structures on four static water levels. The calibration gave different conductivities (Table 10). In structure D both reached the lower limits of their ranges, and the four levels do not determine them, since any lower value would fit the levels at least as well. Structure S matches three of the four levels within 6 m, but it misses borehole 2526AD00180 in the valley by 28.9 m. Structure D fits the four levels through the depth of its ground drain and not through its conductivities. With the regional recharge, structure S fits only with a bedrock transmissivity of 0.60 m²/d, and structure D reaches its fit at the lower limit of 0.10 m²/d. Both are far below the regional 22.81 m²/d.

The 262 archive levels that were not used in the calibration were then compared with both structures. This is a check and not a validation. Structure D has twice as many levels within 10 m as structure S, but it fits slightly worse than the benchmark (Table 10; Figure 12). On the lowest ground the measured water table is deeper than that of both structures, by twice as much in structure S. Structure S places the water table within 5 m of the surface at many more boreholes than the nine where the levels are that shallow. The status of these levels is not recorded, so some may have been measured during pumping. Under the irrigated land the depth to water is 4.5 m in structure S and 15.3 m in structure D (Map 8).

The water budget shows where the recharge goes (Table 10). The two structures receive the same recharge but lose it by different routes. The discharge to the rivers and streams in structure S exceeds every estimate of baseflow, 2.00 to 3.43 Mm³/a (DWS, 2021; Bailey & Pitman, 2016), and the net contribution of the river between the dams (Table 7). In structure D it is below every estimate. The comparison is of scale only, as the quantities are of different kinds. Halving or doubling the recharge, the conductivities and the drain conductances together leaves the water levels unchanged and scales the flows in proportion. Structure S then still exceeds the estimates at half (4.93 Mm³/a), and structure D remains below them at double (1.26 Mm³/a).

The Reserve determination gives a groundwater use of 0.81 Mm³/a against the 0.285 Mm³/a registered, and a baseflow of 2.00 Mm³/a (DWS, 2021). This leaves 14.5 Mm³/a of its recharge of 17.336 Mm³/a unaccounted for, which is five to nine times the estimates of baseflow. The recharge of the model, 17.53 Mm³/a, is slightly more.

## 6.5 Abstraction to offset the shortfall

Abstraction was added under the irrigated land to see how far groundwater could offset the shortfall and where the pumped water would come from. In structure S, the mean drawdown in the 45 pumped cells is 2.30 m at 0.5 Mm³/a and 40.8 m at the registered volume of 2.34 Mm³/a. At this volume the water level in 19 of the cells is deeper than 47.5 m, the base of the weathered zone (Figure 13). At the regional recharge, the drawdown in structure D is five to nine times larger at 0.25 and 0.5 Mm³/a, and any lower conductivity would give more. From 1.0 Mm³/a the pumped cells of structure D cannot deliver the rate asked, and they yield at most about 1.2 Mm³/a. The drawdowns are means over cells of 200 m, and the drawdown in a pumped borehole would be larger.

The source of the pumped water is opposite in the two structures. In structure S, 87% to 89% of the pumped water comes from less discharge to the rivers and streams at rates up to 1.0 Mm³/a, and more than three-quarters at the registered volume. At 0.5 Mm³/a, about 69% comes from the Klein Marico River itself and 20% from the lower Waterkloofspruit. In structure D, the rivers and streams supply under 2%, and the rest comes from the ground drain.

Further cases each changed one part of the set-up (Table 11). With the conductivities of structure S, the drawdown in structure D falls to about that of structure S, but its water still comes entirely from the ground drain. Spreading the abstraction over the surface deposits within 1 km reduces the mean drawdown to a quarter in structure S and to one twenty-eighth in structure D, without changing the source. Abstraction from the bedrock within 1 km lowers the share from the rivers and streams in structure S to 58%. The halved and doubled models fit the four water levels equally well, and their drawdowns in structure S differ by a factor of about eight. In structure D the halved model delivers about 93% of the rate.

## 6.8 Pathways and the two waters

Two things were examined. Particles were released from the 45 model cells under the irrigated land in the runs without abstraction and tracked forward in both model structures (MODPATH; Pollock, 2016), to see where water that recharges under the fields goes. The records of the Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS) were screened against the guidelines of DWAF (1996a, 1996b) to compare the water of the river below Klein Maricopoort Dam with the water generated inside A31E.

In structure S, 387 of the 405 particles (96%) end in a cell where a river drain discharges (Map 10). The median time to reach the river is about 140 years at an effective porosity of 0.10, and it scales with the porosity. In structure D no particle reaches a river or stream, and 288 of the 405 do not leave the cell in which they started. The water table under the fields stands 15.3 m deep in this structure (Map 8).

Yellow dots mark the end of each path. In S, 387 of the 405 paths end at a river; in D none do. The paths show advective flow direction only; contaminant transport is not modelled, and travel times depend on an assumed porosity.

The water-quality records are in Figure 15 and Table 12. Below Klein Maricopoort Dam the median electrical conductivity (EC) was 51.2 mS/m in 1989–1993 (station A3H030, 56 samples) and 62.3 mS/m in 2012–2023 (A3H042Q01, 81 samples), and 86% and 99% of the samples exceeded the upper limit of the target range for irrigation, 40 mS/m. Three sites along the river between the dams (188257, 188038 and 188030; three, three and one samples, 2004–2007) had medians of 76 to 83 mS/m. The Wilgeboomspruit, the one gauged stream inside A31E (1971–1991), had a median of 3.85 mS/m and no exceedance. The groundwater of A31E had a median of 17.7 mS/m in seven samples (DWS, 2021:163), and Kromellenboog Dam, which receives both waters, 21.7 mS/m (1966–2023, 248 samples). Sulphate follows the same order, with medians of 30 to 57 mg/L below Klein Maricopoort Dam and 102 to 145 mg/L along the river between the dams, against 17.7 mg/L in Kromellenboog Dam and 3.05 mg/L in the Wilgeboomspruit. No sample at any site exceeds the guidelines for chloride, sodium or SAR.
