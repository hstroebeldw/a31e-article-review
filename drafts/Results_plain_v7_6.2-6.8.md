## 6.2 The canal and the water of A31E

The inflow to Klein Maricopoort Dam is not gauged, so it was calculated from the dam's own record (series 1). Its median over the hydrological years 1936–2009 is 3.71 Mm³/a (Figure 6). The inflow stayed below the median from 1982 to 1994, and from 1985 to 1994 it averaged 0.84 Mm³/a, about a third of the registered volume before any evaporation. The rainfall was well below the long-term mean in 1982 to 1985 and in 1991, and above it in 1987 to 1990. The dam fell from nearly full in December 1982 to 22% of its capacity in March 1984 and stayed low until the rains of 1995 and 1996 (DWA, 2012).

The water balance shows where the water went (Table 7). Evaporation took a third of the water that entered the dam in 1987–2009 and about half in 1987–1995, when the canal carried only 0.30 Mm³/a. The capacity of 7.07 Mm³ equals about three years of the registered volume, and about two years once the evaporation, net of the rain on the water surface, and the release to the river are included.

The canal record shows how often the canal fell short. In 24 of the 38 years from 1966 to 2009 that have a complete record (63%), the canal carried less than the registered volume, by 58% of that volume on average (Figure 7). The years 1984 to 1996 form one run of thirteen shortfall years (DWS, s.a.b).

To see where the water came from during the drought, the inflow to Kromellenboog Dam was compared with what Klein Maricopoort Dam passed on (Table 8). In 1987–1995 the upper dam passed on almost nothing, and most of the water that reached Kromellenboog Dam came from the river between the dams. Over 1987–2009, 81% of the water that reached Kromellenboog Dam did not come from the upper dam (DWA, 2012; DWS, s.a.b). The Wilgeboomspruit, the one gauged stream inside A31E, was dry in 42 of the 50 months from February 1984 to March 1988 and flowed again from April 1988 (Figure 8).

## 6.3 Reliability of the canal supply

The water balance was tested before it was used, because the inflow is calculated from the same records (Table 9; Figure 9). The restriction rule was fitted on 1966–1974 and 1981–1986 and tested on 1987–2009 (the forward test), and fitted on 1987–2009 and tested on the earlier years (the reverse test). In the forward test the simulated storage and the yearly canal volume meet all three criteria, and the monthly canal volume does not. In the reverse test the monthly canal volume meets the criteria and the yearly canal volume narrowly does not. The yearly canal volume is too high in the forward test and too low in the reverse test.

**Table 9 (new): Test of the water balance in both directions.** In each test the rule was fitted on the other period: 1966–1974 and 1981–1986 for the forward test, and 1987–2009 for the reverse test. The criteria are NSE above 0.5, RSR at most 0.7 and PBIAS within ±25% (Moriasi et al., 2007:891), applied here by analogy. A positive PBIAS means that the model is too low (Moriasi et al., 2007:888). The storage is not compared in the reverse test, because the area curve comes from the survey of 1982. The seasonal pattern is that of Figure 3 in the forward test and that of the years tested in the reverse test.

The reliability of the canal was then calculated against the registered volume over the 74 years (Table 10). With the rule, the canal receives the registered volume in 59% of the months and in 45% of the years, and without restriction in 87% and 77%. With the rule the canal fails about three times as often, but a failure month still brings half of the registered volume, while without restriction the canal then receives an eighth.

With the rule, the drought forms one failure event from December 1983 to January 1996, the longest of the record. Its shortfall is more than nine times the registered yearly volume, and it includes 40% of all failure months (Figure 10). Without restriction, the same drought breaks into separate events of no more than 20 months.

The rule costs water. The canal receives 0.21 Mm³/a less on average, and the dam loses 0.21 Mm³/a more to evaporation and spill. The reliability also depends on the period used to fit the rule: fitted on the fit period alone, the rule gives a reliability by time of 0.59, and fitted on the test period alone it gives 0.39 and a longest event of 169 months.

The column headings "(lower bound)" and "(upper bound)" become "with the rule" and "without restriction".

The adjusted inflow series (series 2) gives almost the same reliability, 0.588 with the rule and 0.865 without.

The historic firm yield is the largest demand that the dam could have met in every month of the record without restriction. It is about 0.36 Mm³/a (Figure 11). One month fails at 0.37 Mm³/a, in October 1992, when the dam held 0.2 Mm³. At 1.5 Mm³/a the unrestricted canal fails in 8% of the months. The rule was fitted at the registered volume, so at lower demands the curve of the rule in Figure 11 is the rule as fitted.

## 6.4 Groundwater model: fit and water budget

The groundwater model was calibrated separately for the two structures on four static water levels, which gave different conductivities (Table 11). Structure S matches three of the four levels within 6 m, but it misses borehole 2526AD00180 in the valley by 28.9 m. In structure D both conductivities lie at the lower limits of their ranges, and the model fits the four levels through the depth of its ground drain. The transmissivity of the bedrock is far below the regional value in both structures.

The 262 archive levels that were not used in the calibration were then compared with both structures. Structure D has twice as many levels within 10 m as structure S, but it fits slightly worse than the benchmark (Table 11; Figure 12). On the lowest ground the measured water table is deeper than that of both structures, by twice as much in structure S. Structure S places the water table within 5 m of the surface at many more boreholes than the nine where the levels are that shallow. Under the irrigated land the depth to water is 4.5 m in structure S and 15.3 m in structure D (Map 7).

The water budget shows where the recharge goes (Table 11). The two structures receive the same recharge but lose it by different routes. The discharge to the rivers and streams in structure S exceeds every estimate of baseflow, which ranges from 2.00 Mm³/a in the Reserve determination (DWS, 2021) to 3.43 Mm³/a by the method of Hughes in the WR2012 study (Bailey & Pitman, 2016), and the net contribution of the river between the dams (Table 8). In structure D it is below every estimate. The comparison is of scale only, as the quantities are of different kinds.

The Reserve determination gives a recharge of 17.336 Mm³/a, a groundwater use of 0.81 Mm³/a and a baseflow of 2.00 Mm³/a (DWS, 2021). This leaves 14.5 Mm³/a of the recharge unaccounted for. The recharge is five to nine times the estimates of baseflow. The recharge of the model, 17.53 Mm³/a, is slightly more.

## 6.5 Abstraction to offset the shortfall

Abstraction was added under the irrigated land to see how far groundwater could offset the shortfall and where the pumped water would come from. In structure S the mean drawdown in the 45 pumped cells is 2.30 m at 0.5 Mm³/a and 40.8 m at the registered volume of 2.34 Mm³/a (Figure 13). In structure D the drawdown is five and nine times larger at 0.25 and 0.5 Mm³/a, and from 1.0 Mm³/a the pumped cells deliver at most about 1.2 Mm³/a.

The source of the pumped water is opposite in the two structures. In structure S, 88% to 89% of the pumped water comes from less discharge to the rivers and streams at rates up to 1.0 Mm³/a, and more than three-quarters at the registered volume. At 0.5 Mm³/a, about 69% comes from the Klein Marico River itself and 20% from the lower Waterkloofspruit. In structure D, the rivers and streams supply under 2%, and the rest comes from the ground drain.

Further cases each changed one part of the set-up (Table 12). With the conductivities of structure S, the drawdown in structure D falls to about that of structure S, but its water still comes entirely from the ground drain. Spreading the abstraction over the surface deposits within 1 km reduces the mean drawdown to a quarter in structure S and to one twenty-eighth in structure D, without changing the source. Abstraction from the bedrock within 1 km lowers the share from the rivers and streams in structure S to 58%. The halved and doubled models fit the four water levels equally well, and their drawdowns in structure S differ by a factor of about eight.

In structure S it lies between the runs at 1.0 and 2.34 Mm³/a.

## 6.6 Climate change

The two climate cases of Table 6 were applied to the water balance of the dam and to the groundwater model, to test how sensitive the canal supply and the groundwater are to the two sensitivity cases for about 2050. Case M is the median step and case D the dry end of the projections. The reliability of the canal supply is measured against the registered volume raised by the demand factor of the case (Section 5.4.4). Table 13 gives the results against the Reference run.

The reliability by time falls in each step, with and without the rule, and the mean shortfall and the longest event grow. With the rule, the reliability by time falls from 0.59 in the Reference run to 0.54 in case M and 0.48 in case D. The dam delivers about as much water in case M as in the Reference run and less in case D (1.79 against 1.86 Mm³/a with the rule).

## 6.7 Flood extent and depth in the modelled reach

The design flood at F2 was estimated with the SDF method (Section 5.2.3), and two model runs were routed through the 15.7 km reach, to find how large the floods are and how much of the irrigated land they cover.

The catchment above the reach is underlain for 46.4% by dolomite of the Malmani Subgroup, all of which lies above Klein Maricopoort Dam, while A31E itself contains none (Michaluk & Keyser, 1991; Walraven, 1981). At the 1:50-year and 1:100-year return periods the SDF gives peaks of 974 and 1 252 m³/s (Table 14).

Over the reach the peak decreases by 1% to 2% and arrives about two hours later. The flooded area changes much less than the peak. ## 6.8 Pathways and the two waters

Two things were examined. Particles were released from the 45 model cells under the irrigated land in the runs without abstraction and tracked forward in both model structures (MODPATH; Pollock, 2016), to see where water that recharges under the fields goes. The records of the Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS) were screened against the guidelines of DWAF (1996a, 1996b) to compare the water of the river below Klein Maricopoort Dam with the water of A31E, which is the Wilgeboomspruit and the seven groundwater samples.

In structure S, 387 of the 405 particles (96%) end in a cell where a river drain discharges (Map 10). The median travel time is about 140 years at an effective porosity of 0.10, and it scales with the porosity. In structure D no particle reaches a river or stream, and 288 of the 405 do not leave the cell in which they started.

**Map 10** (unchanged): Flow paths of 405 particles released in the 45 model cells under the irrigated land (MODPATH 7; Pollock, 2016), for (a) structure S and (b) structure D. Yellow dots mark the end of each path. In S, 387 of the 405 paths end at a river; in D none do. The paths show advective flow direction only; contaminant transport is not modelled, and travel times depend on an assumed porosity.

The water-quality records are in Figure 15 and Table 15. Below Klein Maricopoort Dam the median electrical conductivity (EC) was 51.2 mS/m in 1989–1993 (station A3H030) and 62.3 mS/m in 2012–2023 (A3H042Q01), and 86% and 99% of the samples exceeded the upper limit of the target range for irrigation, 40 mS/m. The three sites along the river between the dams (188257, 188038 and 188030, with three, three and one sample in 2004–2007) had medians of 76 to 83 mS/m.

The Wilgeboomspruit, the one gauged stream inside A31E (1971–1991), had a median of 3.85 mS/m and no exceedance, and the seven groundwater samples of A31E (DWS, 2021:163) a median of 17.7 mS/m. Kromellenboog Dam, which receives both waters, lies between them and the river below the dam at 21.7 mS/m (1966–2023). The order of the sites is the same for sulphate. No sample at any site exceeds the guidelines for chloride, sodium or SAR, and SAR is screened against 2.0, the value for the crop yield (DWAF, 1996a:134). Phosphate-P exceeds 0.025 mg/L, the boundary of a trophic class, in 19% of the samples below the dam, 29% in Kromellenboog Dam and 20% in the Wilgeboomspruit, so the nutrients do not separate the two waters in the same way.

**Figure 15** (unchanged) and **Table 15** (was Table 12, unchanged), as in the current article.

The DRASTIC index was taken from the national map to rate how vulnerable the aquifer is to contamination from the surface. It is low over 96.7% of A31E and moderate over 3.3% (Map 11; DWAF, cited by Esterhuyse, 2017).
