Storage, drought and climate change: the reliability of the canal
supply from Klein Maricopoort Dam and the role of groundwater in
A31E, North West Province

7

Abstract
Irrigation in quaternary catchment A31E, North West Province, depends on a canal from Klein
Maricopoort Dam, which carried less than the registered volume of 2.34 Mm³/a in thirteen
successive years from 1984. This desktop study assessed how reliable the canal supply is, how
far groundwater within A31E could offset its shortfalls and how both respond to a drier climate,
and it screened the floods and contaminant paths along the irrigated reach. A monthly water
balance of the dam (1936–2009) was run with and without a restriction rule, and a steady-state
groundwater model was run in two structures that differ in the depth of outflow. With the rule, the
canal delivers the registered volume in 45% of the years, and without restriction in 77%. In one
structure, pumped groundwater would come mostly from the river that feeds Kromellenboog
Dam. In the sensitivity cases for about 2050, the reliability of the canal is lower, most in the dry
case. The 1:100-year flood, estimated with the SDF method, covers about a quarter of the
irrigated land in the strip below the dam, and the modelled reach reduces its peak by only 1% to
2%. The river below the dam is above the salinity target for irrigation in most samples. The
registered volume cannot be assured by the dam, and groundwater cannot yet be relied on as a
backup, because its effect on the river has not been established.
Keywords: canal supply reliability; reservoir water balance; multi-year drought; groundwater
flow model; capture; climate change; design flood; particle tracking

1. Introduction
In semi-arid regions, irrigated agriculture depends largely on water that has been stored.
Irrigators in a semi-arid South African catchment have coped with past droughts mainly through
the construction of water storage and the adoption of more efficient irrigation (Lankford et al.,
2023). The security of an irrigation supply depends on how reliably a dam can deliver water,
which is described by how often the supply fails, how quickly it recovers and how severe a failure
is (Hashimoto et al., 1982). In the Marico area of the North West Province, irrigation was the
largest water use in 2000, at approximately 32 million cubic metres per year (Mm³/a), and most
of this water was drawn from surface water below dams such as Marico-Bosveld and Klein
Maricopoort (DWAF, 2004:ii, 52).
In South Africa, the quaternary catchment serves as the fundamental hydrological unit for
managing water resources (DWS, s.a.d). In semi-arid quaternary catchments, river flow and
groundwater recharge do not respond to rainfall in simple proportion. Where about 500 mm of
rain falls per year, a decrease of 10% in rainfall may reduce perennial surface drainage by half
(De Wit & Stankiewicz, 2006), and groundwater in drylands is recharged episodically, often
through focused recharge from ephemeral flows after intense rain (Cuthbert et al., 2019). Dry
years tend to occur in runs, and six of the eight most severe two-year droughts in southern Africa
between 1901 and 1999 ended between 1983 and 1996 (Rouault & Richard, 2005). During such
dry periods, groundwater sustains river flow as baseflow, especially in dry seasons (Xie et al.,
2024). Consequently, water availability in a quaternary catchment is strongly influenced by the
sequence of wet and dry years and by the interaction between surface water and groundwater.
Groundwater is a crucial water resource in many semi-arid regions, because it buffers the effects
of rainfall variability (Calow et al., 2010). In South Africa, groundwater contributes approximately
15% of the water consumed nationally (DWAF, cited by Knüppe, 2011:69), but in the Marico area
it supplied 6 of the 32 Mm³/a used for irrigation (DWAF, 2004:52). Over-abstraction has already
lowered groundwater levels in the dolomite aquifers of the North West Province (Cobbing, 2018).
Its use for irrigation is further limited by the yield of boreholes, since yields above 5 L/s are not
widespread in Africa (MacDonald et al., 2012).
The A31E quaternary catchment is a semi-arid catchment in which irrigation depends on stored
surface water. The largest part of its registered water use is not groundwater, but water that
appears to be supplied by a canal from Klein Maricopoort Dam, which stands on the upstream
boundary of the catchment (DWAF, 2004:50; DWS, s.a.f). A failure of this supply directly affects
the water users in the catchment, and it raises the question of whether groundwater within A31E
could sustain them through such a failure.

1.1 Research problem
Klein Maricopoort Dam stores water that is generated in the catchments above A31E. Of the
3.38 Mm³/a of water use registered in A31E, 2.34 Mm³/a (69%) is registered as scheme use on
442 ha within about 15 km of the dam wall, while 0.285 Mm³/a is registered from boreholes

9

(DWS, s.a.f). These scheme records are inferred to represent the users of the canal, because
the Department lists a canal scheme for the dam and the land mapped as irrigated lies below it
(DFFE, 2024; DWS, 2024).
The records show that this supply has failed for extended periods. Between October 1983 and
September 1995, the dam stood below 25% of its capacity in 139 of the 144 months, and this
period is referred to as the drought throughout this study. During these years the canal carried a
mean of 0.36 Mm³/a, compared with a median of 2.02 Mm³/a over 72 years of record (DWA,
2012; DWS, s.a.b). The inflow to the dam did not recover when the rainfall did: it stayed below its
long-term median from 1982 to 1994, although the rainfall of 1986–1995 averaged 99% of its
long-term mean (Bailey & Pitman, 2016). The Department reports a yield of 1.4 Mm³/a for the
dam, without a stated method and with very low confidence in the hydrological information of the
Marico (DWAF, 2004:50, 83). How often, for how long and by how much the deliveries of the
canal fall below the registered volume is not known.
It is equally uncertain whether groundwater within A31E could serve as an alternative source.
The estimated recharge of 17.336 Mm³/a is far larger than the current groundwater use (DWS,
2021). The mapped borehole yields are low, each borehole has a single recorded water level,
and no pumping tests or water-level series exist (DWS, s.a.c). The only gauged stream inside
A31E was dry in 42 of the 50 months from February 1984 (DWS, s.a.b), which suggests that
groundwater storage declined during the same drought that emptied the dam.
Climate change adds a further uncertainty. Warming is projected with considerably more
confidence than any change in rainfall (DEA, 2013), and droughts are projected to become more
frequent and longer (Trisos et al., 2022). For 2041–2060, the climate models assessed by the
IPCC project a warming of about 1.5 to 2.9 °C for eastern southern Africa (ESAF), the region in
which A31E lies, and a change in annual rainfall of about −8.5% to +3%, with a median of about
−3% (10th to 90th percentiles; calculated from Gutiérrez et al., 2021; Iturbide et al., 2020). The
problem addressed in this study is that the reliability of the canal supply, the extent to which
groundwater could offset its shortfalls and the sensitivity of both to climate change have not been
established. Water users and the responsible authorities lack a quantified basis for drought
planning.

1.2 Aim, objectives and research questions
The aim of this study is to quantify the reliability of the canal supply from Klein Maricopoort Dam,
the extent to which groundwater within A31E could offset its shortfalls and the response of both
to climate change, so that drought planning in the catchment can rest on the records rather than
on a registered volume. The study is a desktop assessment of existing records, intended for the
Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS), the North West Premier's Office and the consulting
partner AGES. Flood risk along one reach of the Klein Marico River and the pathways along
which contaminants could move are screened, and the transport of contaminants is not
modelled.
The objectives of the study are:

10

1. To compile and review the existing hydrological, geohydrological and water-use information
for A31E, identify its gaps, and develop a conceptual model of how water moves through the
catchment.
2. To simulate the water balance of Klein Maricopoort Dam and its canal and quantify the
reliability of supply against the registered scheme volume.
3. To assess with a groundwater flow model how far abstraction within A31E could offset
shortfalls in the canal supply, and the limits that the data place on this assessment.
4. To assess the effects of climate change by about 2050 on the reliability of the canal supply
and on groundwater.
5. To screen the flood risk along the irrigated reach of the Klein Marico River and the pathways
along which contaminants from the land could reach the rivers.
6. To provide evidence-based recommendations, including priorities for data collection, to the
DWS, the North West Premier's Office and AGES.
The research questions are:
1. How reliable is the canal supply from Klein Maricopoort Dam to irrigators in quaternary
catchment A31E under the historical sequence of wet and dry years, to what extent could
groundwater within A31E offset the shortfalls that arise when the dam is drawn down, and
how would both change under plausible climate conditions for about 2050?
2. How large are the floods along the irrigated reach, and along which paths could contaminants
from the land reach the receptors?

2. Study Area
2.1 Location and the two dams
Quaternary catchment A31E lies in the North West Province, east of Zeerust, and covers
approximately 602 km² (Bailey & Pitman, 2016). It drains to the Marico River, part of the upper
Limpopo River basin (Jury, 2016), within the Limpopo-Olifants Water Management Area (DWS,
2023). The Klein Marico River enters A31E at the wall of Klein Maricopoort Dam and leaves the
catchment through Kromellenboog Dam (Map 1). The reservoir of the upper dam and its whole
drainage area lie outside the catchment, and none of the runoff generated within A31E can reach
Klein Maricopoort Dam. The water stored in the upper dam comes entirely from outside A31E,
while the runoff of A31E flows to Kromellenboog Dam, which is operated for an irrigation scheme
in the downstream catchment A31F (DWAF, 2004:50, 57).

Map 1: Locality of quaternary catchment A31E, showing the Klein Marico River, Klein Maricopoort Dam at the
upstream boundary, Kromellenboog Dam at the outlet, the upstream catchments A31D and A31C, Zeerust and
the main roads. Inset: position in the North West Province.

The canal leaves the upper dam at its wall, and the Department lists an irrigation scheme for the
dam that is supplied “from the canal system” (DWS, 2024). The dam also releases water to the
river. It was raised in 1966, and its survey of 1982 gives a capacity of 7.07 Mm³ at full supply
(DWA, 2012).

2.2 Climate
The climate of the Marico area is semi-arid, with rainfall that is “strongly seasonal with rainfall
occurring as thunderstorms in summer” (DWAF, 2004:18). The mean annual precipitation (MAP)
of A31E is 597 mm in the Water Resources of South Africa, 2012 study (WR2012), which takes
its rainfall from the series of rainfall zone A3B (Bailey & Pitman, 2016:14). The mean annual
evaporation of the evaporation zone of A31E is 1 901 mm (Bailey & Pitman, 2016).

2.3 Topography and drainage
A31E falls from the high ground in the south, where the Waterkloofspruit and the
Wilgeboomspruit rise, to Kromellenboog Dam in the north-east. A valley crosses the northern
part of the catchment from the Doringrivier in the west to the dam (Map 2). Four streams drain
A31E. The Klein Marico River flows from the wall of Klein Maricopoort Dam through the valley to
Kromellenboog Dam. The Doringrivier drains the western arm and joins it below the dam wall,
the Waterkloofspruit joins it further downstream, and the Wilgeboomspruit reaches it at the head
of Kromellenboog Dam.

Klein Marico River. The stations at the wall of Klein Maricopoort Dam lie within 400 m of one another, and only
some are labelled.

2.4 Geology and hydrogeology
A31E is underlain by rocks of the Pretoria Group, which are cut by diabase and partly covered
by surface deposits (Map 3; Michaluk & Keyser, 1991; Walraven, 1981). Quartzite and
sandstone, mainly of the Timeball Hill Formation, form the high ground in the south. The
andesitic lava of the Hekpoort Formation lies in the east, mudrock in the centre-east and hornfels
south-west of the centre, and diabase occurs in small bodies. Surface deposits, including

13

alluvium, lie mainly along the valley in the north. Groundwater occurrence depends primarily on
geology, weathering and effective rainfall (MacDonald et al., 2012:2). On the hydrogeological
map, A31E is classified as an intergranular and fractured aquifer and a fractured aquifer, and
karst aquifers do not occur in A31E. The mapped median yield of boreholes is 0.5–2.0 L/s over
most of A31E and 0.1–0.5 L/s in the rest (Map 4; Barnard, 1999). The National Groundwater
Archive (NGA) holds the borehole records of the catchment (DWS, s.a.c). The Department
judged that groundwater in the Marico can meet domestic supplies "but not bulk water
requirements for purposes such as irrigated agriculture" (DWAF, 2004:55–56).

2.5 Soils and land cover
The soils of A31E are assigned to the hydrological soil groups B, B/C, C and C/D, which rank
soils according to their runoff potential when thoroughly wet, from moderately low in group B to
high in group D (Schulze & Schütte, 2023; USDA-NRCS, 2009:7-2). Group C/D lies in the northeast and along the northern edge, group B/C in a belt from the west across the centre, group C
in the south, and group B in one small patch in the north-east (Map 5). Natural woodland covers
most of A31E, with natural grassland in the south and west. Fallow land, old fields and cultivated
land lie mainly along the valley, with small patches of irrigated land, and plantation forest lies in
the south. Kromellenboog Dam lies in the north-east (Map 6; DFFE, 2024).

3. Literature foundation
3.1 Reliability of supply from a small dam
The performance of a water-supply system is judged by more than the average volume that it
delivers. Hashimoto et al. (1982:14–17) proposed three criteria for this purpose. Reliability
describes how often the system is in a satisfactory state, resiliency describes how quickly it
recovers once it has failed, and vulnerability describes how severe a failure is. All three criteria
depend on a threshold that separates a satisfactory delivery from an unsatisfactory one. In this
study, a failure is a period in which the canal delivers less than the registered scheme volume, so
that the criteria answer directly how often, for how long and by how much deliveries fall short.
Reliability of the supply is not the same as the resilience of the irrigators, who may plant a
smaller area or apply less water when the supply decreases (Lankford et al., 2023).
A small dam can carry a scheme through one dry year, but not through a run of dry years. The
relationship between rainfall and runoff can shift during a multi-year drought (Saft et al., 2015),
so that a relationship calibrated on wetter years may overestimate the inflow in precisely the
years that determine reliability. Evaporation adds to this problem, since the loss increases with
the surface area of the water and is expected to increase further as the climate warms, and it is
often overlooked in the water accounting of small reservoirs (Aminzadeh et al., 2024). The
registered volume is used as the threshold, because it is the volume that the scheme is entitled
to receive, but it differs from both the water gauged at the head of the canal and the water used
on the fields (Section 6.1).
The only planning assessment found for the dam is the Department’s strategy report for the
Marico, which gives a yield without a stated method, does not include the dam in its proposed
systems model and does not mention the drought at the dam (DWAF, 2004:50, 83–84). Its
finding that the Marico sub-area was “in balance” while the Reserve was not implemented
(DWAF, 2004:53, 93) concerns the whole sub-area, and it says nothing about how often the
supply of this particular dam fails.

3.2 Groundwater as a backup, and modelling with few data
Groundwater security during drought depends on the water stored in an aquifer, on access to
this water through boreholes and on the demand placed on it (Calow et al., 2010:246). A source
that is reliable for domestic supply may fail under the larger demand of irrigation. Sustainable
groundwater use should be based on capture, which is the decrease in discharge and increase
in recharge that pumping brings about, rather than on recharge alone (Seward et al., 2006:473).
Where groundwater discharges to rivers, capture is largely a loss of streamflow, and across the
United States it supplied about 0.85 of long-term withdrawals, with a mean of 0.61 in the 34
aquifer systems and areas that Konikow and Leake (2014) reviewed. This loss arrives later than
the pumping and continues after it stops (Leake & Barlow, 2013), so that a balance of recharge

18

and use cannot show which discharge an abstraction would reduce. In A31E, access to
groundwater appears to be the main limitation, because the irrigated land lies on surface
deposits of unknown thickness in the lower mapped yield class (Section 6.1).
Where records are sparse, models are often calibrated over short periods. Ebrahim et al. (2019)
calibrated a groundwater model of the hard-rock Hout catchment over five years and estimated
the unmetered pumping from satellite data. A model calibrated over a few years may not
represent a drought that is longer than its calibration period. For Klein Maricopoort Dam, the
monthly reservoir record covers 1936 to 2010 and includes the long drought, and the simplest
defensible approach uses this record directly. For groundwater the records are too few to identify
a single conceptual model, and several should then be carried forward (Barnett et al., 2012:26),
because calibrated models that differ little in their heads can differ greatly in their predictions
(Troldborg et al., 2007:843).

3.3 Climate change and the canal supply
The projections used in this study are those assessed by the IPCC in its Sixth Assessment
Report. The Atlas of this report gives regional results for the global models of the sixth Coupled
Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6) and for the regional models of CORDEX-Africa
(Gutiérrez et al., 2021). A31E lies in the reference region of ESAF (Iturbide et al., 2020), about 1°
east of its boundary with western southern Africa. The projections for ESAF are used because
the catchment lies inside this region, but its regional mean is weighted towards the wetter east.
For 2041–2060, the CMIP6 models under an intermediate emission scenario (SSP2- 4.5) give a
median change of about −10% in the rainfall of September to November, and 91% of the models
are drier (calculated from Gutiérrez et al., 2021; Iturbide et al., 2020, 2022). The IPCC also
projects an increase in agricultural and ecological drought in southern Africa (Ranasinghe et al.,
2021:1771).
Projections that were downscaled for South Africa are less uniform. The Green Book projects
that the warming may exceed 3 °C over the northern parts of the country by 2021–2050 under
high emissions, and it projects more rain over the central interior but drier conditions in the northeast in most of its runs (Engelbrecht, 2019:12, 37). The ensembles agree on strong warming and
differ on rainfall. Because perennial drainage responds more than proportionally to rainfall where
about 500 mm falls per year (De Wit & Stankiewicz, 2006), even a modest drying could reduce
the inflow to the dam by a larger fraction, which is an inference of this study. The wettest tenth of
the CMIP6 models gives about +0.5% to +3% more annual rain (calculated from Gutiérrez et al.,
2021). The climate cases of this study are drawn from the median and the dry end of the
projections.

3.4 Design floods and contaminant pathways
Design floods in South Africa are estimated by three groups of methods (Smithers, 2012:634).
Statistical methods analyse observed floods, deterministic methods convert a design rainfall into
a flood, and empirical methods are based on the largest floods recorded in a region. Statistical
analysis requires a record of observed floods that is “adequate in both length and quality”
(Smithers, 2012:635), which the monthly records of A31E do not contain. The Standard Design

19

Flood (SDF) is a deterministic method that was developed from the flood records of 152 gauging
stations in 29 drainage basins (SANRAL, 2013:3-43, 3-44). It exceeded statistical estimates by
60% on average and is regarded as conservative (Smithers, 2012:639), and it is the design flood
used in this study.
Contaminants are approached through the source–pathway–receptor model, in which water is at
risk only where a source releases a contaminant, a pathway carries it and a receptor receives it
(Mbae et al., 2024). The pathway through the ground can be traced by particle tracking. The
program MODPATH follows particles through the flows calculated by MODFLOW and derives
their times of travel from the porosity of the material (Pollock, 2016:2–3). Particle tracking shows
where water goes and roughly how long it takes, but not how a dissolved substance spreads or
reacts.
The literature provides the criteria, the reasons why multi-year droughts and evaporation govern
a small dam, a projected range of climate change and methods for screening floods and
pathways. It does not provide a measure of how often, for how long and by how much the
deliveries from Klein Maricopoort Dam fall short, how this would change by about 2050, or
whether groundwater in A31E could make up part of a shortfall. This study addresses that gap.

4. Conceptual model
4.1 The system and its two sources of water
The system studied is the land between two dams, together with the upper dam that supplies it
(Figure 1). Water enters this system in two ways, which are referred to as the two waters. River
flow from the catchments upstream is caught in Klein Maricopoort Dam, and rain falls on A31E
itself. The first water feeds the canal and the irrigated land, while the second can reach neither
the upper dam nor the canal. Path 1 carries the water from outside A31E through the upper dam,
where evaporation removes water but not the salts dissolved in it, to the canal and the river. Path
2 begins with the rain on A31E and runs over the ground to the streams or down to the
groundwater. The two paths meet below the canal, on the irrigated land, where canal water
drains to the river and the groundwater, and in Kromellenboog Dam, which receives both. The
inflow to the upper dam is taken as recorded and serves as the condition at the upstream
boundary.

Figure 1: Schematic section of A31E along the Klein Marico River, from the wall of Klein Maricopoort Dam to
Kromellenboog Dam, showing the water that enters from outside A31E (orange) and the water from rain on
A31E itself (blue). Quantities are means for hydrological years 1987–2009; not to scale.

4.2 The upper dam and the canal
Water leaves the upper dam by four routes, namely evaporation, the canal, the spill and the
release to the river, and no supply to Zeerust appears in the record. Beyond the gauge at its
head, the canal is not measured. Its losses, the fields that it serves and the water that returns to
the river or sinks to the groundwater are unknown, but none of this water can return to the upper
dam. In a year of median inflow, a delivery close to the registered volume lowers the storage,

21

while in a wet year most of the water spills, because the dam is already full. The storage of the
dam and the inflow from upstream respond over several years, and these slow parts of the
system decide whether the canal can deliver.

4.3 Rain on A31E, groundwater and the lower dam
The runoff of A31E is gathered by the tributaries and ends in Kromellenboog Dam. Most of the
rain that does not run off evaporates, and a small part recharges the groundwater, which is
stored mainly in the weathered zone. That this groundwater moves from the high ground towards
the valleys is an inference from four single water levels (DWS, s.a.c). Groundwater leaves the
catchment through boreholes, as discharge to the streams, by uptake of vegetation and possibly
as underground outflow (DWS, 2021).
Kromellenboog Dam receives the water of both paths. The net contribution of the reach between
the dams is mostly runoff and groundwater discharge of A31E, but it also contains any canal
water that returns to the river from the fields, less the water abstracted from the river.

4.4 Contaminant sources, pathways and receptors, and floods
The same conceptual model shows the routes along which dissolved substances would travel
(Section 3.4). Two main sources can be identified. The first is the return flow from Zeerust, from
irrigation in A31D and from the mines around Zeerust, whose nutrient and salt content has
limited its re-use (DWAF, 2004:57). Since Zeerust and A31D lie above the dam (Map 1), this
return flow is inferred to enter the river upstream of it. The second is the irrigated land below the
dam, where water not used by crops carries salts, and anything applied to the fields, back to the
river or down to the groundwater. The rest of the catchment had a population of only 936 in 2011
and contains no large urban source (DWS, 2021:156).
These sources reach the receptors along path 1 and path 2. Below the fields, groundwater can
discharge to the river only where the water table stands above the river bed, and where it stands
deeper, the water leaves by evaporation and uptake by plants instead. Whether the water table
stands above the river bed separates the two model structures of Section 5.2.2.
Four receptors follow from these paths: the users of the canal, who receive path 1 water; the
groundwater under the irrigated land, which this study assesses as a backup to the canal and for
which no quality record exists; the river between the dams; and Kromellenboog Dam, which
receives both paths. The river between the dams is an ecological flow site whose category, C
(moderately modified), is to be maintained (DWS, 2021:130; Kleynhans & Louw, 2007:A2-11).
The DRASTIC index indicates how easily water from the land surface reaches the groundwater
(Aller et al., 1987:13). The index is a relative evaluation tool that does not replace on-site
investigation (Aller et al., 1987:11, 20), and it says nothing about the surface deposits in
particular.
Floods follow the same two paths. A flood from upstream enters as spill over the wall of the
upper dam, the tributaries add floods from A31E itself, and Kromellenboog Dam receives both.
The Department identified the spillway of Klein Maricopoort Dam as “a major risk” and the
spillway capacity of Kromellenboog Dam as “a major risk factor” (DWAF, 2004:170–171).

5. Methodology
The methodology is described in four steps, namely the data, their preparation, their processing
within software and their analysis (Sections 5.1 to 5.4). Each step deals in turn with the water
balance of the dam, groundwater, floods, and water quality and climate. All the methods rest on
the conceptual model of Section 4, and the water balance uses monthly records.

5.1 Data
5.1.1 Dam water balance
The station records of the Department give one value per month with a quality code (DWS,
s.a.b). The reservoir record gives the storage, outflows, evaporation and rain of Klein
Maricopoort Dam for every month from 1936 to 2010 (DWA, 2012), and the WR2012 study
supplies the rainfall of zone A3B and the patched inflow (Bailey & Pitman, 2016). Evaporation at
the dam is measured with a Symons pan (S-pan). The inflow is not gauged but calculated from

generated inside A31E is that of the Wilgeboomspruit (station A3H016), which drains 39 km² and
ends in 1992 (DWS, s.a.b). The national register of water use (DWS, s.a.f) records entitlements

Kromellenboog Dam downstream, the spill record of 1977 to 1979, and the canal record of 2018
to 2021, which repeats one flow rate (DWA, 2012; DWS, s.a.b).

A 20 m digital elevation model (DEM) was made in ArcGIS Pro from spot heights and contours. It
gives the top of the groundwater model, the ground levels of the boreholes and the levels of the

The model removes water from every cell through a ground drain, which represents seepage,
springs, evaporation and uptake by plants. Two depths of the ground drain are used, and they
define the two structures of the model: structure S, with a shallow ground drain, and structure D,
with a deep one. In structure S the ground drain lies 1 m below the land surface. In structure D it
lies 15.27 m below the surface, which is the median depth to water of 112 archive levels in A31E
and its neighbouring catchments (DWS, s.a.c). These 112 levels include the four calibration
levels but none of the 262 levels that are later compared with the model. The depth of 15.27 m is
also close to the mean maximum rooting depth of tropical grassland and savanna, 15.0 m

for comparison afterwards. The recharge is the regional rate of 29.2 mm/a. The benchmark for
the fit is a water table 15.27 m below the ground everywhere.
The abstraction is placed in GIS in three ways. Placement P1 spreads it over the 45 model cells
under the land mapped as irrigated, P2 over the 474 cells of surface deposits within 1 km of that
land, and P3 over the 410 bedrock cells within the same distance. Because the effective porosity
of A31E is not known, a porosity of 0.10 is used for the particle times, which lies within the range
published for fine sand, silt and clay, the surface deposits in which 41 of the 45 cells under the
irrigated land lie (Woessner & Poeter, 2020).

5.3 Data processing within software
5.3.1 Dam water balance
The monthly water balance of the dam and its canal was built in Microsoft Excel from the station
and reservoir records. It is a mass balance of one reservoir with a restriction rule, which is the
form that Adeloye et al. (2016) use to calculate reliability, resilience and vulnerability for a single
dam. A simpler storage–yield calculation would leave out evaporation, while a river-system
model such as WEAP (Dlamini et al., 2023) would add components for which no records exist.
In each month, the storage at the end equals the storage at the start, plus the inflow and the rain
on the water surface, minus evaporation, the release to the river, the canal delivery and the spill.
The balance runs over the 888 months from October 1936 to September 2010 and applies the
present capacity of 7.07 Mm³ to every year. It describes the present dam and registered volume
under the inflows of the past and is not a reconstruction of history. The canal receives no water
once the storage reaches 0.166 Mm³, which is the lowest storage since the survey of 1982.

5.3.2 Groundwater
The shares of rock group, aquifer type, yield class and soil group, and all elevations, were
measured in ArcGIS Pro by overlaying the mapped layers on the boundary of A31E and on the
land mapped as irrigated.
The groundwater model was built in GMS 10.9.6 and run with MODFLOW-NWT (Niswonger et
al., 2011). It consists of one layer, 207.5 m thick, with its top at the 20 m DEM, and 15 142 active
cells of about 200 m by 200 m (145 rows and 222 columns). The recharge of 29.2 mm/a (17.53
Mm³/a) is applied to every cell, and the outer edge is closed. Water leaves the model only
through drains, which remove water whenever the water level stands above them (Harbaugh,
2005). River drains follow the four named rivers at the level of the lowest land beside the
channel, and stream drains follow all other mapped streams at the land surface, both with a
conductance of 10 m²/d per metre of channel. The ground drain of every cell has a conductance
of 0.05 m²/d per m².
The hydraulic conductivity has two zones, surface deposits (3 479 cells) and bedrock (11 663
cells). It was estimated for each structure with PEST (Doherty & Hunt, 2010) within the limits
0.03–3.0 m/d for the deposits and 0.0005–0.05 m/d for the bedrock, using the four static water
levels with an interval of 10 m each. The recharge was held at its regional value, because
recharge and conductivity cannot be separated in a calibration on heads alone (Knowling &
Werner, 2016). The calibrated model without wells is the base case of each structure.
A31E has no water-level series for a transient model, so the model is run at steady state, in
which the water entering the aquifer equals the water leaving it, and the cases are compared
with one another. The residual of a borehole is its measured water level minus the simulated
one, and the fit is expressed as the root-mean-square error of the residuals.
Particle tracking used MODPATH 7 (Pollock, 2016) in the base case of each structure. Nine
particles were released at the water table in each of the 45 cells under the irrigated land, 405 in
all, and tracked forward. The effective porosity was entered as 1.0 in every cell, and the travel

31

times are scaled to a porosity of 0.10 afterwards. Each particle was classed by the drains of the
cell in which it ended.

5.3.3 Flood
The floods were routed with HEC-RAS 7.0.1 in an unsteady two-dimensional model (USACE
HEC, s.a.a), in which the water can spread in any direction over the valley floor as the flood
passes. The model area is 24.3 km², with a mesh of 30 m cells refined to 15 m along the river.
Manning’s n, which describes how strongly the land surface resists the flow, was applied by landcover group. The upstream boundary at F2 is the inflow hydrograph, with an energy slope from
the terrain, and the downstream boundary is normal depth. The default diffusion-wave equations
were used. The hydrographs have values every 15 minutes, with a minimum of 5 m³/s.

balance and the groundwater model described above.

5.4 Analysis
5.4.1 Dam water balance
To pass the test, the simulated monthly storage and yearly canal volume had to meet the criteria
of Moriasi et al. (2007:891) for a satisfactory simulation, which were set for streamflow and are
applied here by analogy. These criteria are a Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) above 0.50, a ratio
of the root-mean-square error to the standard deviation of the observations (RSR) of 0.70 or
less, and a percent bias (PBIAS) within ±25%. The inflow itself cannot be tested, because it is
calculated from the storage record. For the same reason, the fit of the monthly storage mainly
tests the restriction rule and the outflows, while the yearly canal volume is the more independent
test. The test is independent only for the restriction rule and the demand, because the area
curve, the pan factors and the release table were derived from the test period itself, and passing
a split test is in any case “a necessary, rather than a sufficient, condition” for a model to be
adequate (Klemeš, 1986:13). After the test, the rule was refitted on both periods together, so that
the rule used in all further runs also contains the test period. Its shares rise from 0.036 in the
lowest zone to 1.00 above 57% of capacity. The second, adjusted inflow series was run as a
check. The rule was also fitted on each period alone, to show how far the reliability depends on
the period used. In the reverse test the rule was fitted on 1987–2009 and tested on 1966–1974
and 1981–1986, with the storage at the start of each period taken from the record, and the same
three criteria were applied to the 178 months and the 14 years with a counted canal volume.
The supply is judged by the criteria of Hashimoto et al. (1982), calculated with the formulas of
Adeloye et al. (2016). A month fails when the delivery is below 99% of the registered volume of
that month. Reliability by time is the share of months without failure, reliability by volume the
share of the registered volume delivered, and yearly reliability the share of years with the full
volume. Resilience is the number of shortfall events, which are unbroken runs of failure months,
divided by the number of failure months, and vulnerability is the mean depth of a shortfall. The
shortfall of a month is the registered volume less the simulated delivery at the head of the canal,

32

and it is referred to as the shortfall against the registered volume throughout. Every criterion is a
frequency counted in one record of 74 years and not a probability, since synthetic series
(Kjeldsen & Rosbjerg, 2004) would require a stochastic model of the inflow. A curve of reliability
against demand was run for demands of 0.5 to 3.0 Mm³/a. The historic firm yield is the largest
demand that the dam could have met in every month of the record without restriction.
The records cannot distinguish a month in which the canal was restricted from a month in which
less water was wanted, and every run is made twice. With the restriction rule, the canal receives
the share given by the rule, while without restriction it receives the full volume for as long as
water remains in the dam. The unrestricted run gives the upper bound of reliability, because
every restricted month counts as a failure and much of the water held back in the dam is lost to
evaporation. The run with the rule gives the lower bound for the scheme as it was operated on
average over the record, but it is not the lowest possible value, since a rule that restricts more
would lower the reliability further (Section 6.3). For resilience and vulnerability the order is
reversed, because a restricted canal fails more often but less deeply, and the two bounds are
always reported together. The sensitivity of the results was tested by changing one input at a
time (Pianosi et al., 2016:216), namely the inflow series, evaporation, the capacity, the release to
the river, the rule and the seasonal pattern of demand.

5.4.2 Groundwater
The shortfall was first compared with the groundwater balance of the Reserve determination
(DWS, 2021). Such a balance treats recharge as water that is free to be taken, while pumped
water is balanced mostly by capture (Section 3.2), and only a flow model can show which
discharge is reduced by pumping. With four static water levels and a steady-state calibration, the
model belongs to Class 1, the lowest class of confidence in the Australian groundwater
modelling guidelines, whose uses include developing coarse relationships between abstraction
and impact (Barnett et al., 2012:20–21). Both structures are needed because four static water
levels cannot identify a single conceptual model (Section 3.2). The fit was compared with the
benchmark, and the 262 other archive levels were compared with both structures afterwards.
Neither comparison is a validation (SKM, 2013:18).
Abstraction was run at placement P1 at 0.25, 0.5, 1.0 and 2.34 Mm³/a in structure S and at 0.25,
0.5, 1.0, 2.0 and 2.34 Mm³/a in structure D. Placement P2 was run at 0.5 Mm³/a in both
structures and at 2.34 Mm³/a in structure S, and placement P3 at 0.5 Mm³/a in structure S, to
test whether spreading the abstraction changes the result. The recharge was also multiplied by
the factors of cases M and D, 0.96 and 0.89, both without wells and with 0.5 Mm³/a at P1.
Three results are read from each case and compared with the base case at the same recharge.
These are the rate actually pumped, because MODFLOW-NWT reduces the rate of a well when
its cell nearly dries (Niswonger et al., 2011), the drawdown in the pumped cells, and the change
in the outflow of each group of drains as a share of the rate pumped. The drawdown is the fall of
the water table caused by the abstraction, and the lowering is the difference in the water table
between two runs with the same abstraction, one at the base recharge and one at the reduced
recharge. Both are means of 200 m model cells.

33

A steady-state drawdown is the state reached if pumping continued indefinitely at the mean
recharge. It exceeds the drawdown of a single season of pumping at the same recharge, and
during a long drought, when the recharge falls below its mean, it would be larger. The loss of
streamflow would in reality arrive later and continue after pumping stops (Section 3.2). Two
further comparisons show what the four water levels cannot decide. In one, the recharge, the
conductivities and the drain conductances are all halved or all doubled, which gives the same
water levels and so fits the four levels equally well. In the other, structure D is run with the
conductivities of structure S. The mean shortfall and the rate needed in the longest shortfall
event were expressed as shares of the recharge of the Reserve determination and compared
with the rates that the model could deliver.
The particle times are those of the moving water alone, without dispersion, retention or decay.
They scale in proportion to the effective porosity, which was not measured, and a porosity of
0.01 would make them ten times shorter than at 0.10.

5.4.3 Flood
The design flood was estimated with the SDF method of the drainage manual (SANRAL, 2013).
Two plans were run, the 1:50-year and the 1:100-year flood. Two simplifications act in opposite
directions, since ignoring the attenuation in Klein Maricopoort Dam makes the flood larger, while
leaving out the inflow of the Waterkloofspruit makes the flood below its junction smaller. A cell of
the mesh was counted as flooded where its maximum depth was at least 0.01 m. The peak
reduction is the fall of the peak between the inflow at F2 and the outflow at the downstream
boundary, and the delay is the time between the two peaks. The irrigated land flooded is the area
of land mapped as irrigated (DFFE, 2024) that lies in the flooded cells, calculated in a GIS.

5.4.4 Water quality and climate
Each water-quality quantity was compared with its guideline value, and the share of samples
above the guideline was counted for each site. A value above a guideline shows that the water
could affect its use or its ecosystem, but it does not show that the water is contaminated. The
guideline defines the trophic classes for the average summer concentration, so the share of
single samples above a boundary is a screening indication and not a trophic classification
(DWAF, 1996b:84, 97).
In the climate cases, the dam was run with the inflow multiplied by 0.93 and 0.80 and
evaporation and demand by 1.064. The canal is asked for the registered volume raised by the
same factor, 2.49 Mm³/a, and the reliability is measured against this volume. The groundwater
model was run with the recharge multiplied by 0.96 and 0.89 (Section 5.4.2). The simulated
storage of the cases was compared by the share of months in which it stood at or above each
level.

6.1 The irrigated land and the records
A31E falls from 1 567 m in the south to 1 038 m at Kromellenboog Dam (Map 2). Between the
two dams, the river falls about 104 m over a distance of 24.7 km. The Doringrivier joins the Klein
Marico River 4.2 km below the dam wall and the Waterkloofspruit 13.7 km below it. The water
surface of the upper dam stands at about 1 150 m, while all the land mapped as irrigated below
it lies lower, between 1 093 and 1 140 m, so that a canal leaving the dam could reach this land
by gravity.
The rock types of Map 3 are combined into four groups: quartzite and sandstone cover 31.8% of
A31E, the andesitic lava of the Hekpoort Formation and diabase 24.4%, mudrock, shale and
hornfels 20.9%, and surface deposits 22.5%. On the hydrogeological map, 59.8% of A31E is
classified as an intergranular and fractured aquifer and 40.2% as a fractured aquifer. Of the land
mapped as irrigated, 91.6% lies on surface deposits. The thickness and composition of these
deposits are not known. The mapped median borehole yield is 0.5–2.0 L/s over 75.3% of A31E
and 0.1–0.5 L/s over the remaining area, which includes three-quarters of the irrigated land. The
registered scheme volume is equal to about 74 L/s pumped continuously, which would require
about 150 to 740 boreholes at yields of 0.5 to 0.1 L/s. According to the modelled regional values
of the GRAII (Section 5.1.2), the weathered zone holds about 91% of the water that would drain
if both zones were dewatered (storativity multiplied by thickness).
Each of the 266 boreholes with a water level has a single level. The 262 levels that are not
recorded as static have a median depth of 19.4 m, and the median strike yield of 109 boreholes
is 0.3 L/s.
The soils of A31E belong to the hydrological soil groups B/C (38.7%), C (31.5%), C/D (28.7%)
and B (1.2%). 83.2% of the land mapped as irrigated lies on group B/C. The Wilgeboomspruit
yielded about 53 mm/a, or 7.6% of the rainfall, in the six complete years of 1966–1980 (DWS,
s.a.b). Natural woodland and grassland cover 87% of A31E and cultivated land 10.2%, more
than half of which is fallow. Land mapped as irrigated covers 164.9 ha in 18 patches in the strip
below the dam, of which 147 ha are under centre pivots (Map 6). Built-up land covers 0.3%.
Half of the scheme positions of the register lie on higher ground above the level of the dam,
which a canal cannot reach by gravity. Three different quantities describe irrigation from the dam.
The register lists 442 ha and 2.34 Mm³/a, the land cover maps 165 ha of irrigated land, of which
147 ha is under centre pivots, and the canal carried 1.89 to 2.16 Mm³/a in 2022 and 2024 (DWS,
s.a.b).

6.8 Contaminant pathways and water quality
Two things were examined. Particles were released from the 45 model cells under the irrigated

In structure S, 387 of the 405 particles (96%) end in a cell where a river drain discharges (Map

the porosity. In structure D no particle reaches a river or stream, and 288 of the 405 do not leave
the cell in which they started.
The water-quality records are in Figure 15 and Table 15. Below Klein Maricopoort Dam the
median electrical conductivity (EC) was 51.2 mS/m in 1989–1993 (station A3H030, 56 samples)

7. Discussion
The canal supply from Klein Maricopoort Dam cannot be relied on for the registered volume. The
dam is small in relation to that volume, so a drought of several years empties it, and the manner
in which deliveries are restricted as it empties sets the balance between how often the supply
fails and how severe a failure is (Hashimoto et al., 1982:18–19). Why the inflow remained low
during the drought is not yet settled. The historic firm yield of the dam is also well below the 1.4
Mm³/a that the Department gives, a figure for which the report states no method and which rests
on hydrology that has not been updated since 1988 (DWAF, 2004:50, 54, 83).
The drought further shows that A31E carries two waters, which differ in origin and in quality. The
water in the canal comes from outside the catchment; it fell short during the drought, and in most
samples its salinity is above the target for irrigation. The Wilgeboomspruit and the groundwater
of A31E are much fresher, but this water enters the river below the canal and therefore does not
reach it.
Because groundwater is tied to both waters, it can back up the canal only in part. Water pumped
from an aquifer often comes from less discharge elsewhere (Leake & Barlow, 2013). In structure
S it comes mostly from the Klein Marico River, and so from the water that feeds Kromellenboog
Dam. Which structure is closer to the catchment the records cannot yet show: the 262 other
water levels favour structure D on the share within 10 m, but neither structure fits them better
than a constant depth to water. The same question decides where water from the irrigated fields
goes, since in structure S nearly all of it would reach a river, whereas in structure D none would.
Climate change adds to these limits. In the two sensitivity cases for about 2050, the canal supply
is less reliable than in the Reference run, most of all in the dry case, and in both structures the
discharge of groundwater to the rivers and streams falls by about as much as the recharge.
Planning should therefore be tested against the dry end of the projections rather than against the
median.
For part of the irrigated land, shortage is not the only risk. This land lies on the floodplain, so its
users face both a shortage that can last for years and flooding. The model shows that about a
quarter of the irrigated land in the strip is covered by the 1:100-year flood.
Taken together, planning in the catchment should assume droughts of several years rather than
the registered volume, and groundwater cannot yet be relied on as a backup, because its effect
on the river has not been established. Each remaining range has a cause that data could
narrow: for the canal, the restriction rule, and for groundwater, the position of the water table
relative to the river bed.

55

8. Recommendations
Each recommendation follows from the Discussion (Section 7) and names its addressees.
1. Plan for shortfalls that last several years (DWS; Premier's Office). Drought plans should
treat the registered volume as a volume that the dam cannot assure, and should be tested
against the dry end of the climate projections and not the median.
2. Review the yield that the Department gives for the dam (DWS). The report that gives the
yield states no method, and the Marico hydrology has not been updated since 1988. The
historic firm yield found here is well below it.
3. State the restriction rule of the scheme and make it known to the irrigators (DWS). The
records do not show which rule was applied, and the rule sets the balance between how often
and how badly the canal fails.
4. Do not rely on groundwater as a backup until its effect on the river has been assessed
(DWS; Premier's Office; AGES). In one structure of the model most of an abstraction comes
from the river that supplies Kromellenboog Dam.
5. Monitor the quality of the river below the dam, the inflow to the dam and boreholes
below the irrigated land (DWS; AGES). The river below the dam is above the salinity target
for irrigation in most samples, and the source of its salt and the quality of the groundwater
below the fields are not known.
6. Collect the data that would reduce the uncertainty most. The restriction rule and the
position of the water table relative to the river bed decide most of the ranges, so the priorities
are:
(a) The operating and restriction rule of the scheme, with yearly records of the water
ordered and delivered (DWS).
(b) A gauged record of the inflow to Klein Maricopoort Dam and of the water used above it,
to settle the cause of the low inflow (DWS).
(c) Water levels and electrical conductivity in boreholes beside the Klein Marico River
below the dam (DWS; AGES).
(d) The lithological logs of the valley deposits in the archive, and pumping tests in those
deposits (AGES).
(e) Water-level series in the valley and in the western arm (AGES).

56

9. Conclusions
This study set out to quantify the reliability of the canal supply from Klein Maricopoort Dam, how
far groundwater within A31E could offset its shortfalls and how both respond to a drier climate,
and to screen the floods and the contaminant paths along the irrigated reach.
The canal supply is not reliable for the registered volume. The dam is small against it, and one
drought of many years, in which the inflow stayed low, left the canal short year after year.
Groundwater within A31E cannot yet be relied on as a backup. In one structure of the model
most of the pumped water would come from the river that feeds Kromellenboog Dam, and the
water levels available cannot show which structure is closer to the catchment. In the sensitivity
cases for about 2050, the canal supply and the discharge of groundwater to the rivers are both
lower, most in the dry case.
Part of the irrigated land lies on the floodplain and is covered by the 1:100-year flood. Water from
under the irrigated land would reach a river in one structure and not in the other. The river below
the dam is above the salinity target for irrigation in most samples, and the water of A31E is
fresher.
The registered volume cannot be assured by the dam, and the recommendations follow from
this: plan for droughts of several years, review the yield, state the restriction rule, and collect the
data that would narrow the two main unknowns.
