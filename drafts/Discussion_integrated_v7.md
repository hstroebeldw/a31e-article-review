## 7. Discussion

The canal supply cannot be relied on for the registered volume. The dam is small against that volume, and in the drought the inflow was just over a third of it for twelve years. Why the inflow stayed low is not settled: it may have come from recharge that stayed low (Pyburn, 2015:113, citing an earlier study) or from greater use of water upstream (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:128). The water balance reproduces the storage of the dam and the yearly canal volume in the test that looks forward.

The restriction rule matters more for the reliability than the size of the demand, so it is the first thing to settle. Restricting deliveries as the dam empties makes the failures more frequent and longer but less deep, and it delivers less water in total, as in a simulated reservoir (Hashimoto et al., 1982:19). The records do not show which restriction was applied. Without restriction the firm yield is about a quarter of the 1.4 Mm³/a that the Department gives, a figure that rests on hydrology not updated since 1988 (DWAF, 2004:50, 83).

The drought also shows that A31E carries two waters that behave differently. The water that the dam releases comes from outside A31E, failed in the drought, and in most samples below the dam is above the salinity target for irrigation. The runoff and the groundwater of A31E are much fresher. Most of the water that reached Kromellenboog Dam in the drought came from the river between the dams, but it enters below the canal and could not replace the canal supply.

Groundwater is tied to both waters, and this limits it as a backup. The shortfall is a small share of the recharge, but pumped water often comes from less discharge elsewhere (Leake & Barlow, 2013). In structure S, pumping under the irrigated land takes mostly water that would otherwise have flowed down the river towards Kromellenboog Dam, and in structure D the pumped cells cannot deliver the larger rates. The records cannot show which structure is closer to the catchment, so the result is a range. The same unknown decides where water from the irrigated fields goes: in structure S nearly all of it would reach a river, and in structure D none would, because the ground drain removes it in the cell where it enters.

A warmer and probably somewhat drier climate weakens the canal supply and, in structure D, the groundwater backup, so planning should be tested against the dry end of the projections and not the median. The projections point to more agricultural and ecological drought in southern Africa (Ranasinghe et al., 2021:1771).

Part of the irrigated land lies on the floodplain, so its users face two risks: a shortage of water that lasts for years, and flooding. The size of the design flood depends mainly on the choice of method, and the dolomite above the dam may make the SDF too high (SANRAL, 2013:3-45). The flooded extent is a screening and not a flood line. The state of the spillways of both dams is not known to this study.

For the catchment, this means that planning should assume droughts of several years and not the registered volume, and that groundwater cannot yet be relied on as a backup, because its effect on the river has not been established. Each range has a known cause that data could narrow: the unknown restriction rule for the canal, and the unknown position of the water table relative to the river bed for groundwater.
