## 6.2 The canal and the water of A31E

The inflow to Klein Maricopoort Dam, calculated from the dam's own record (series 1), has a median of 3.71 Mm³/a over the hydrological years 1936–2009, or 3.22 Mm³/a without the doubtful years 1975–1980 (Figure 6). It stayed below both medians from 1982 to 1994, although the rainfall of 1987 to 1990 was above its long-term mean, and over the ten years from 1985 to 1994 it averaged 0.84 Mm³/a, which is about a third of the registered volume before any evaporation. The dam fell from 99% of its capacity in December 1982 to 22% in March 1984, and it stayed low until the rains of 1995 and 1996 (DWA, 2012).

Evaporation took a third of the water that entered the dam over 1987–2009 and about half in 1987–1995, when the canal carried only 0.30 Mm³/a (Table 6). The survey capacity of 7.07 Mm³ equals about three years of the registered scheme volume, and about 1.8 years once the evaporation at the mean storage of 1987–2009 and the release to the river are added to that volume.

Of the 38 years of the raised dam, from 1966 to 2009, with a complete canal record, the canal carried less than the registered volume in 24 years (63%), with a mean shortfall in those years of 58% of the registered volume (Figure 7). The longest run of shortfall years lasted ten years, from 1984 to 1993; 1994 is not counted because one month has no value (DWS, s.a.b). In five of the shortfall years the dam never fell below 75% of its capacity.

The reach between the dams supplied the water that Klein Maricopoort Dam did not pass on. Its net contribution is the inflow to Kromellenboog Dam less the spill and release of the upper dam. It is net of river abstraction and includes return flow of canal water, which the records cannot separate (Table 7). Over 1987–2009 the net contribution was 81% of the inflow to Kromellenboog Dam, 98% in 1987–1995 and 75% in 1996–2009, although the zone rainfall of the two periods was almost the same. It is positive in every year, and it has a rank correlation of 0.76 with the zone rainfall over 21 years (DWA, 2012; DWS, s.a.b). Because the canal carried far less water in 1987–1995 than later, part of the rise after 1995 may be return flow of canal water.

The Wilgeboomspruit, the only gauged stream inside A31E, flowed in every dry-season month (May to September) on record before February 1984 and averaged 2.1 Mm³/a from December 1965 to January 1984. It was dry in 42 of the 50 months from February 1984 to March 1988, and three of the four months in this period with more than 100 mm of zone rainfall had no flow (211 mm in February 1988). It flowed again from April 1988 and was dry again from March 1992. From April 1988 to August 1992 it averaged 0.69 Mm³/a, a third of its earlier flow, while the zone rainfall of the months with a valid record fell from 660 to 496 mm/a (means annualised from the months with a valid value; Figure 8).

## 6.3 Reliability of the canal supply

In the test period of 1987–2009, the simulated monthly storage and the yearly canal volume meet all three criteria, and the monthly canal volume does not (Table 8; Figure 9). The balance delivers 22% more water than the canal carried, and about half of the difference falls in hydrological years 1995 and 1996, the two years after the drought, in which together the balance gives about three times the canal volume. The balance finds a shortfall in 192 of the 265 months with a valid canal record, against 216 in the record.

Over the 74 years, the canal receives the full registered volume in 59% to 87% of the months, and a year passes without a shortfall in 45% to 77% of the years (Table 9). With the rule, the canal fails three times as often, but a failure month still brings half of the registered volume, while without restriction the canal then receives an eighth. With the rule, the drought forms a single event from December 1983 to January 1996, with a total shortfall of more than nine times the yearly volume, and it includes 40% of all failure months (Figure 10). Without restriction, the same drought breaks into separate events of no more than 20 months. With the rule the canal receives 1.86 Mm³/a on average and without restriction 2.07 Mm³/a, and the dam under the rule loses an extra 0.15 Mm³/a to evaporation and 0.06 Mm³/a to spill. The rule fitted on the fit period alone gives a reliability by time of 0.59, and the rule fitted on the test period alone gives 0.39 and a longest event of 169 months. The adjusted inflow series, prepared for the years before 1987, changes the reliability by time to 0.588 with the rule and 0.865 without restriction.

Without restriction, a lower demand helps (Figure 11). The largest demand that the dam could have met in every month of the record is about 0.36 Mm³/a on series 1, since no month fails at 0.36 Mm³/a and one month fails at 0.37 Mm³/a, and about 0.30 Mm³/a on the adjusted series. In both, the first month to fail is October 1992, when the dam held 0.2 Mm³. At 1.5 Mm³/a the unrestricted canal fails in 8% of the months. With the rule, no firm yield exists, because the canal is restricted whenever the dam stands low. Even at a demand of 0.5 Mm³/a the rule leaves a reliability by time of 0.82 and an event of 128 months, because it restricts delivery by storage whatever the demand. The difference between the two bounds at the registered volume, 0.28 in reliability by time, is about as large as the change from raising the demand from 0.5 to 3.0 Mm³/a with the rule (0.29).

## 7.1 The canal supply in drought

The two bounds represent two ways of running short of water, in which a restricted canal fails more often but less deeply. Hashimoto et al. (1982:19) found a similar trade-off in a simulated reservoir, where “high system reliability was accompanied by high system vulnerability”. In the model, restriction leaves less water for the canal, partly because the dam holds more water and loses more of it to evaporation and spill. The rule run is not a strict lower bound. It is the rule fitted on both periods of the record, and the periods give different rules, so that the test period alone gives a lower reliability. The rule also cannot separate a restriction by the operator from irrigators asking for less water. The reliability depends about as much on the rule as on the demand, and the result is therefore a range that is conditional on the rule.

The test shows consistency and not validation. The inflow is calculated from the storage record, so the fit of the monthly storage mainly tests the rule and the outflows, and the area curve, the pan factors and the release table were derived from the same period. The yearly canal volume is the more independent test, and it passes narrowly. The monthly canal volume does not pass, so the timing of single failures is uncertain. The longest event is the same under both inflow series, and its length is set by how long the storage stays in the restricted zones of the rule. The simulated reliabilities are probably optimistic in the years after the drought, when the model delivered about three times what the canal carried, either because the operator kept restricting or because irrigators asked for less.

The reliabilities describe the security of an entitlement and not the water used by crops. The register records entitlements (DWS, s.a.f), and the registered area, the mapped irrigated land and the volume in the canal are quantities of different kinds that cannot be combined. The canal record alone overstates how often the supply failed, because in some shortfall years the dam stood above three-quarters of its capacity and the canal carried less than the registered volume for reasons other than a lack of water. Asking for the registered volume in every year avoids this in the unrestricted run, but the rule run carries it forward, because the rule is fitted on delivered against asked-for volume. Once evaporation and release are counted, the capacity of the dam covers less than two years of the registered volume, and the drought lasted twelve years.

The historic firm yield is about a quarter of the 1.4 Mm³/a that the Department gives as the yield of the dam (DWAF, 2004:50), but the two cannot be assumed to be alike. A yield is normally stated at an assurance of supply, which the historic firm yield does not have, since it is the largest draft that one recorded sequence allowed. It is set by a single month and shifts with the inflow series. The Department gives no method for its figure and reports that the hydrological information of the Marico has not been updated since the agreement of 1988 and is of very low confidence (DWAF, 2004:83).

One drought dominates the counts under both bounds, so its cause determines how they should be interpreted. Two things need explaining: why the inflow fell, and why it stayed low. The fall coincides with the dry years of 1981 to 1986, but the low inflow persisted after the rainfall recovered, which points to a store that had to refill before it could sustain flow again. Springs of the Zeerust dolomite declined to almost nil in 1986 and stayed low until the rains of 1996 (Pyburn, 2015:45), and the low flow of 1983 to 1995 is attributed to little recharge (Pyburn, 2015:113). The Wilgeboomspruit, which has no dolomite, was also dry until 1988 and recovered only partly. A greater use of water upstream is a second explanation, since the Rietpoort Well Field began to abstract in 1974 and the Rietpoort Spring ceased flowing about ten years later, in a period of low rainfall (Pyburn, 2015:5). Abstraction and drought act on the same store, and the records cannot separate them, which is common where gauged flows are affected by poorly defined developments upstream (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:128). That the water of these springs reaches the dam is inferred from the position of the dolomite above it, and groundwater divides in dyke-bounded compartments need not follow surface divides. The return of the inflow to above its median after 1996 limits how much of the fall can be due to a lasting increase in upstream use. The same two kinds of cause are argued where runoff did not recover after a drought: in 37% of 161 Victorian catchments the ratio of runoff to rainfall had not recovered by 2017 after the Millennium Drought (Peterson et al., 2021:745), and declining groundwater storage was rated among the plausible explanations, farm dams only as a possible contributor (Fowler et al., 2022). The inflow is calculated from the storage record, so it carries the errors of every other term of the balance, and its monthly values at low storage are the least certain. The drought is a single event in a record of 74 years, so its recurrence cannot be estimated. The two causes also have opposite consequences: if the store has memory, a run of dry years is followed by years of low inflow, and if upstream use has grown, the earlier record overstates the inflow of today. The reliabilities remain frequencies counted in one record.
