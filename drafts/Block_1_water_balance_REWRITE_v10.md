## 6.2 The canal and the water of A31E

The inflow to Klein Maricopoort Dam, calculated from the dam's own record (series 1), has a median of 3.71 Mm³/a over the hydrological years 1936–2009 (Figure 6). The rainfall of 1982 to 1985 was between 347 and 454 mm/a, well below the long-term mean of 599 mm, and the inflow stayed below the median from 1982 to 1994, although the rainfall of 1987 to 1990 was above the mean. Over the ten years from 1985 to 1994 the inflow averaged 0.84 Mm³/a, which is about a third of the registered volume before any evaporation. The dam fell from 99% of its capacity in December 1982 to 22% in March 1984, and it stayed low until the rains of 1995 and 1996 (DWA, 2012).

Evaporation took a third of the water that entered the dam over 1987–2009 and about half in 1987–1995, when the canal carried only 0.30 Mm³/a (Table 6). The capacity of 7.07 Mm³ equals about three years of the registered volume, and about 1.8 years once the evaporation at the mean storage and the release to the river are added to that volume.

Of the 38 years of the raised dam, from 1966 to 2009, with a complete canal record, the canal carried less than the registered volume in 24 years (63%), by 58% of the registered volume on average (Figure 7). Ten of the shortfall years form one run, from 1984 to 1993. The record of 1994 lacks one month, but 1994 also fell short, as did 1995 and 1996, so the run lasted thirteen years (DWS, s.a.b). In five of the shortfall years the dam never fell below 75% of its capacity.

The reach between the dams supplied the water that Klein Maricopoort Dam did not pass on (Table 7). Its net contribution is the inflow to Kromellenboog Dam less the spill and release of the upper dam. It was 3.80 Mm³/a in 1987–1995 and 5.80 Mm³/a in 1996–2009 under almost the same rainfall, although the modelled naturalised runoff of A31E was higher in the first period. It is positive in each of the 21 years with a value, and it has a rank correlation of 0.76 with the zone rainfall (DWA, 2012; DWS, s.a.b).

The Wilgeboomspruit, the only gauged stream inside A31E, flowed in every dry-season month (May to September) on record before February 1984, and it averaged 2.1 Mm³/a from December 1965 to January 1984. It was dry in 42 of the 50 months from February 1984 to March 1988, including February 1988, when the zone rainfall was 211 mm. It flowed again from April 1988 but was dry again from March 1992 to the end of the record in August 1992. From April 1988 to August 1992 it averaged 0.69 Mm³/a, a third of its earlier flow, while the annualised zone rainfall of the months with a valid record was 660 mm/a before and 496 mm/a after (Figure 8).

## 6.3 Reliability of the canal supply

The water balance was tested in two directions (Table 8; Figure 9). In the forward test, over 1987–2009, the simulated monthly storage and yearly canal volume meet all three criteria and the monthly canal volume does not. In the reverse test, over 1966–1974 and 1981–1986, the monthly canal volume meets the criteria and the yearly canal volume narrowly does not. The balance delivers 22% more water than the canal carried in the forward test and 19% less in the reverse test. About half of the forward difference falls in hydrological years 1995 and 1996, the two years after the drought, in which together the balance gives about three times the canal volume. The balance finds a shortfall in 192 of the 265 months with a valid canal record, against 216 in the record.

**Table 8 (new): Test of the water balance in both directions.** In each test the rule was fitted on the other period: 1966–1974 and 1981–1986 for the forward test, and 1987–2009 for the reverse test. The criteria are NSE above 0.5, RSR at most 0.7 and PBIAS within ±25% (Moriasi et al., 2007:891), set for monthly streamflow and applied here by analogy. A positive PBIAS means that the model is too low (Moriasi et al., 2007:888). The seasonal pattern of each test comes from the canal record, so the monthly canal volume is not independent of it. In the reverse test the canal carried more than the registered volume in 9 of the 14 years, which the model cannot deliver, and the storage is not compared, because the area curve comes from the survey of 1982.

Over the 74 years, the canal receives the full registered volume in 59% to 87% of the months, and a year passes without a shortfall in 45% to 77% of the years (Table 9). With the rule, the canal fails three times as often, but a failure month still brings half of the registered volume, while without restriction the canal then receives an eighth.

With the rule, the drought forms a single event from December 1983 to January 1996, with a shortfall of more than nine times the yearly volume, and it includes 40% of all failure months (Figure 10). Without restriction, the same drought breaks into separate events of no more than 20 months. The rule costs water: the canal receives 1.86 Mm³/a on average and without restriction 2.07 Mm³/a, because the dam under the rule loses an extra 0.15 Mm³/a to evaporation and 0.06 Mm³/a to spill. The reliability depends on the period used to fit the rule. Fitted on the fit period alone, the rule gives a reliability by time of 0.59, and fitted on the test period alone it gives 0.39 and a longest event of 169 months. The adjusted inflow series, prepared for the years before 1987, leaves the reliability by time almost unchanged.

Without restriction, a lower demand helps (Figure 11). The historic firm yield, the largest demand that the dam could have met in every month of the record, is about 0.36 Mm³/a on series 1, since no month fails at 0.36 Mm³/a and one month fails at 0.37 Mm³/a, and about 0.30 Mm³/a on the adjusted series. In both, the first month to fail is October 1992, when the dam held 0.2 Mm³. The yield rises to about 0.44 Mm³/a if the lowest usable storage is set at zero instead of the lowest storage on record (0.17 Mm³). It is about a quarter of the 1.4 Mm³/a that the Department gives for the dam (DWAF, 2004:50, 54). At 1.5 Mm³/a the unrestricted canal fails in 8% of the months. With the rule, no firm yield exists, and even at a demand of 0.5 Mm³/a the rule leaves a reliability by time of 0.82 and an event of 128 months.

## 7.1 The canal supply in drought

The canal supply is not secure through a drought as long as that of 1983 to 1995, and how far it falls short depends on how the canal was restricted. With the rule the canal fails more often but less deeply, and without restriction it fails less often but more deeply. Hashimoto et al. (1982:19) found a similar trade-off in a simulated reservoir, where “high system reliability was accompanied by high system vulnerability”. The rule also costs water, because the dam then holds more water and loses more of it to evaporation and spill.

A rule fitted on one period does not reproduce the canal volume of the other period well, so the result with the rule depends on the period used to fit it and is not a strict lower limit. The operator may have restricted harder after 1987, irrigators may have asked for less water, or the demand before 1987 may have been larger than the registered volume, and the records cannot separate these.

The test shows that the model is consistent with the record and not that it is validated, because the inflow was calculated from the recorded storage and no gauged inflow exists. The inflow is the remainder of the dam's own record and carries the errors of every other term, but the fall during the drought is too large to be an error in evaporation.

The reliabilities describe the security of the registered volume and not the water that crops used, because the register records registered and authorised use (DWS, s.a.f). In some shortfall years the dam stood above three-quarters full, so the canal record alone overstates how often water was short. The historic firm yield is well below the figure of the Department, but the two are not like with like. The Department states no assurance of supply for its figure (DWAF, 2004:50), and its hydrological information has not been updated since the agreement of 1988 and is of very low confidence (DWAF, 2004:83).

The inflow probably fell because of the dry years of 1982 to 1985 and stayed low because recharge stayed low for years afterwards. An earlier study cited by Pyburn (2015:113) calculated no or very little recharge between 1983 and 1995, with falling spring flow and water tables, and the flow of the Paardenvallei springs declined to almost nil in 1986 and stayed low until the rains of 1996 (Pyburn, 2015:45). That these springs feed the dam is inferred from the position of the dolomite above it. Greater use of water upstream cannot be excluded, since gauged low flows are often affected by poorly defined upstream developments (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:128). Because the drought is a single event in the record, its recurrence cannot be estimated, and the reliabilities are frequencies counted in one record.
