## 7. Discussion

The canal supply cannot be relied on for the registered volume. The dam is small against that volume, the drought lasted twelve years, and the inflow in those years was just over a third of the volume. The inflow is not gauged but is the remainder of the dam's own record, so it carries every error of that record. Restricting deliveries as the dam empties gives more frequent but shallower failures (Hashimoto et al., 1982:19), and the records do not show which restriction was applied. The rule matters more for the reliability than the size of the demand, so it is the first thing to settle. The firm yield with no restriction is about a quarter of the 1.4 Mm³/a that the Department gives (DWAF, 2004:50, 54). Whether the low inflow came from recharge that stayed low from 1983 to 1995 (Pyburn, 2015:113, citing an earlier study) or from greater use of water upstream (Kapangaziwiri et al., 2011:128) is not settled.

The drought also shows that A31E carries two waters that behave differently. The water that the dam releases comes from outside A31E. It failed in the drought, and in most samples below the dam it is above the salinity target for irrigation, probably because it carries return flows from upstream (DWAF, 2004:57). The runoff and the groundwater of A31E are much fresher. Most of the water that reached Kromellenboog Dam in the drought came from the reach between the dams, but it enters the river below the canal and could not replace the canal supply.

Groundwater is tied to both waters, and this limits it as a backup. The shortfall is a small share of the recharge, but that says little about the water that can be taken, because pumped water often comes from less discharge elsewhere (Leake & Barlow, 2013). In structure S, pumping under the irrigated land takes mostly water that would otherwise have flowed down the river towards Kromellenboog Dam. In structure D, the pumped cells cannot deliver the larger rates. The records cannot show which structure is closer to the catchment, and models that fit alike can predict very differently (Troldborg et al., 2007), so the result is a range. Where boreholes are placed also matters, because spreading them lowers the drawdown in both structures. Water that drains below fields irrigated from the dam would recharge the same aquifer, and no record of groundwater quality exists below the fields.

A warmer and probably drier climate weakens the canal supply and the groundwater backup together, so planning should be tested against the dry end of the projections and not the median. The climate cases probably understate the loss, because they keep the recorded sequence of years and cannot show the longer and more frequent droughts that the projections give (Ranasinghe et al., 2021:1771).

Part of the irrigated land lies on the floodplain, so its users face two risks: a shortage of water that lasts for years, and flooding. The size of the design flood depends mainly on the choice of method, which the records cannot settle, and the dolomite above the dam may make the SDF too high (SANRAL, 2013:3-45). The flooded extent is a screening and not a flood line, because the terrain is coarse and no flood has been recorded in the reach. The present state of the spillways of both dams is not known to this study.

For the catchment, this means that planning should assume droughts of several years and not the registered volume, and that groundwater is a partial backup whose effect on the river has to be assessed first. Each range has a known cause that data could narrow: the unknown restriction rule for the canal, and the unknown position of the water table relative to the river bed for groundwater.
